1 International Operating Standards Chapter 1

One international standard sets the baseline for running an airline across borders: who holds operational control, which alternates exist, what flight time means and how safety is managed.

🎯 Parts 1, 2 and 3🎯 Operational control🎯 Four kinds of alternate🎯 Block-to-block time🎯 Safety management🎯 Navigation lights
💡 Key idea

The operator holds operational control — the authority to start, continue, divert or end a flight. It can delegate that only to the pilot-in-command (or, with an approved supervision method, a flight dispatcher).

Part 1

Aeroplanes in international commercial air transport.

Part 2

Aeroplanes in general aviation.

Part 3

Helicopters in international commercial air transport.

Alternates

AlternateUsed when
Take-off alternateThe departure aerodrome cannot be used shortly after take-off
En route alternateAn abnormal or emergency condition arises en route
ETOPS en route alternateAn engine is shut down on an extended twin operation
Destination alternateLanding at the destination becomes impossible or inadvisable

Safety management and lights

  • 🛡️ The State sets a safety programme; each operator runs its own safety management system.
  • 📊 Aeroplanes over 27 000 kg need a flight data analysis programme.
  • ⏱️ Flight time runs from first movement for take-off until the aeroplane finally comes to rest — block to block.
  • 💡 Navigation lights: red left, green right (each 110°), white tail (140°). Seeing red on your right and green on your left means the other aircraft is heading towards you.
🧠 Memory hook "Red, right, returning": if the red light is on your right, it is coming back at you.

2 General Requirements Chapter 2

The everyday rules of commercial operation: the commander's authority, fitness for duty, documents on board, what stays on the ground, and how leasing changes who the operator is.

🎯 Commander's authority🎯 Alcohol and fitness🎯 Documents on board🎯 Ditching distance🎯 Wet and dry lease
💡 Key idea

The commander is responsible for everyone on board from boarding until leaving, and for the aeroplane from first ready to taxi until it finally stops with the engines shut down.

Fitness for duty

  • 🍷 No alcohol within 8 hours of duty or standby; blood alcohol no more than 0.2 promille.
  • 🤿 Not on duty until a reasonable time after deep diving or blood donation.
  • 🔇 The "sterile" period: take-off, initial climb, final approach and landing — essential duties only.
  • 📼 Flight recorders are never switched off in flight; after an incident the CVR may be stopped to protect the recording.

Documents

Originals

Certificate of registration, certificate of airworthiness, noise certificate, radio licence.

Copies are enough

The AOC and the third-party insurance certificate.

Leasing

Dry lease

Aeroplane only. Flown under the lessee's AOC.

Wet lease

Aeroplane and crew. Flown under the lessor's AOC.

Worked example — ditching distance

An aeroplane with more than 30 passenger seats cruises at 180 kt. The limit is 120 minutes at cruising speed or 400 NM, whichever is less.

120 min at 180 kt = 360 NM, which is less than 400 NM, so it may go 360 NM from land suitable for an emergency landing unless it meets the ditching requirements.

After an incident, flight data and voice recordings are kept for 60 days unless the authority asks for longer.

3 Operator Certification and Supervision Chapter 3

Before carrying a paying passenger, an operator needs an air operator certificate, a set of named managers, a main base, and an operations manual in four parts.

🎯 Who issues the AOC🎯 Accountable manager🎯 Nominated post holders🎯 Special approvals🎯 Operations manual A–D
💡 Key idea

The AOC comes from the State of the operator's principal place of business, and lists the types of operation, aircraft, areas and every special approval (CAT II/III with minima, ETOPS, RVSM, RNAV, dangerous goods).

  • 👔 The accountable manager makes sure operations and maintenance can be financed and done to standard.
  • 🧑‍💼 Four nominated posts: flight operations, maintenance system, crew training, ground operations.
  • 🔁 Authority audits include the operator's internal audits and its financial records.

  • 🛬 Isolated destination (turbine): going on to the nearest alternate would need more than 2 hours of fuel.
  • 📍 Equivalent position: a fix 3 to 5 NM from the threshold, standing in for an outer marker.

4 Operational Procedures Chapter 4

How a flight is planned and flown: alternates and their minima, when an approach may be continued, how visibility becomes RVR, fuel, passengers and reporting.

🎯 Take-off alternate distance🎯 Planning minima🎯 No-alternate rule🎯 Approach ban🎯 Visibility to RVR🎯 Fuel components🎯 Oxygen and reports
💡 Key idea

Planning minima are deliberately higher than operating minima. A plan built on the higher figure leaves room for the weather to get worse before the flight arrives.

Take-off alternate distance

Twin

Within 1 hour at one-engine-inoperative cruise speed (or approved ETOPS time, max 2 h).

Three or four engines

Within 2 hours at one-engine-inoperative cruise speed.

ETOPS en route alternates add 200 ft and 800 m for a precision approach, 400 ft and 1500 m for a non-precision or circling one.

When no destination alternate is needed

  • ⏱️ Flight no longer than 6 hours.
  • 🛬 Two separate usable runways.
  • ☁️ From 1 h before to 1 h after ETA: ceiling at least 2000 ft or circling height + 500 ft (higher), visibility at least 5 km.

The approach ban

⚠️ Below minima An approach may be started whatever the reported RVR, but must not continue past the outer marker (precision) or below 1000 ft (non-precision) if the RVR is below minima. Past that point, it may continue to DA/H or MDA/H.

The touchdown zone RVR always controls. Midpoint minimum 125 m (75 m with roll-out guidance); stop end 75 m.

Twins without ETOPS approval

  • ✈️ Class A, 20 seats or more, or 45 360 kg or more: 60 minutes one-engine-out.
  • ✈️ Smaller Class A: 120 minutes (180 with approval for turbojets).
  • 🛩️ Class B or C: 120 minutes or 300 NM, whichever is less.

Fuel, oxygen and reports

  • ⛽ Taxi + trip + reserve; reserve = contingency + alternate + final reserve + additional (+ extra at the commander's choice).
  • 🚫 No refuelling with wide-cut fuel or Avgas while passengers are on board or boarding.
  • 😷 Crew on oxygen continuously above 13 000 ft cabin altitude, and above 10 000 ft after 30 minutes.
  • 📝 Accident or serious incident report to the authority within 72 hours; ACAS RA and bird strike reports too.
  • 📏 RVSM: 1000 ft separation from FL290 to FL410.
Worked example — planning minima for a non-precision alternate

The alternate has a VOR approach with MDH 400 ft and RVR 1500 m. For planning, add 200 ft and 1000 m: ceiling 600 ft, visibility 2500 m.

VFR minima

  • ☁️ Above 3000 ft AMSL or 1000 ft above terrain: 1500 m horizontally and 1000 ft vertically from cloud; visibility 8 km at or above FL100, 5 km below.
  • 🌁 Special VFR: do not start below 3 km visibility; do not continue below 1.5 km.
  • 👁️ No visual approach with RVR below 800 m.
Worked example — which category?

VS0 is 100 kt. VAT = 100 × 1.3 = 130 kt, which lies between 121 and 140 kt: Category C. Circling minima are 600 ft and 2400 m.

6 Aeroplane Equipment and Instruments Chapter 6

What must be fitted and carried, and the mass and seat thresholds that trigger each item. Most rules switch on at 5700 kg, 9 seats or 19 seats.

🎯 First aid kits🎯 Recorders🎯 Flights over water🎯 ELT frequencies🎯 Supplemental oxygen🎯 Megaphones and axes
💡 Key idea

Learn the thresholds, not every item. More than 5700 kg or more than 9 seats brings in heated pitot, TAWS, standby horizon and weather radar; more than 19 seats brings in the flight deck door, cabin crew, PA and ACAS II (with 5700 kg).

Who may fly, how often they are checked, how many cabin crew are needed, how long records are kept, and how far duty may stretch.

💡 Key idea

Checks run to the end of a month, and the 3-month rule protects early renewal. Pass within the last 3 months of validity and the new period counts from the old expiry date, not from today.

Crew

  • 🧑‍✈️ Two pilots for IFR or night in all turbojets and in turboprops with more than 9 seats.
  • 💺 Cabin crew: one per 50 seats or part of 50, when there are more than 19 seats.
  • 🔁 A cruise relief pilot may act only in the cruise, not below FL200.

Duty and rest

  • ⏱️ Duty: 60 h in 7 days, 190 h in 28 days. Block time: 100 h in 28 days, 900 h in a calendar year.
  • 📉 Basic daily flight duty period 13 h, minus 30 min per sector from the third (6 sectors or more: 11 h).
  • 🌙 WOCL: 02:00–05:59 home base time.
  • 😴 Rest: at least the preceding duty, and never less than 12 h at home or 10 h away, including 8 h of sleep opportunity.
  • 🧭 Commander's discretion: up to 2 h (3 h augmented), reported within 28 days.
Worked example — the 3-month rule

An OPC expires on 31 December. Passed in October (within the last 3 months), the new one runs 6 months from 31 December: 30 June. Passed in August (too early), it runs 6 months plus the rest of August: 28 February.

8 Long Range Flight and Polar Navigation Chapter 8

Far from ground aids, the crew relies on its own systems. Knowing which one is wrong, and keeping it honest, is the whole game.

🎯 Two versus three systems🎯 Independent fix🎯 Revised clearance🎯 Polar problems🎯 Minimum time route
💡 Key idea

Three systems can vote; two can only disagree. With two, an independent fix — radar, a beacon, other aircraft's winds — is needed to decide which one has drifted.

  • 📍 Plot the present position about 10 minutes after each waypoint.
  • 🧲 Above about 65°N, steep dip and changing variation make magnetic references unreliable: grid navigation, inertial and satellite systems take over, and far-north VORs are aligned to true north.
  • 📻 Polar flight also means HF communications, few alternates and large gyro corrections (earth rate, transport wander).
  • 📝 If the aircraft cannot keep to its clearance, get a revised clearance first, using urgency or distress procedures if needed.

Shortest distance

The minor arc of the great circle.

Minimum time route

The quickest track within ATC limits, often longer because of the winds. Computer-generated.

9 North Atlantic High Level Airspace Chapter 9

The busiest oceanic airspace in the world, with few ground aids: tracks that change twice a day, strict navigation standards and contingency procedures everyone flies the same way.

🎯 HLA levels🎯 Boundaries🎯 Organised tracks🎯 Track message identifier🎯 HF and 123.45🎯 Contingencies
💡 Key idea

The high level airspace runs from FL285 to FL420 (formerly MNPS airspace), containing the RVSM levels FL290–410. Aircraft and crews need approval to enter it.

The organised track system

Westbound tracks

Crossing 30°W 1130–1900 UTC.

Eastbound tracks

Crossing 30°W 0100–0800 UTC.

  • 🗺️ Oceanic boundaries at 30°W (Shanwick/Gander), 40°W (Santa Maria/New York) and 45°N between them.
  • 🗓️ The track message identifier is the day of the year: 15 March is 074 (075 in a leap year). Amendments add a letter: 074A.
  • 📍 "59/10" means 59°N 010°W. Reports every 10° of longitude (20° above 70°N).
  • ⚠️ About 80% of gross navigation errors follow a reroute — always cross-check the waypoints.

Communications

  • 📻 HF voice and datalink; long-range VHF within about 250 NM of land.
  • 🗣️ Monitor 121.5 MHz, and 123.45 MHz air-to-air.
  • 🕰️ Oceanic clearance at least 40 minutes before entry; revise the entry estimate if it changes by 3 minutes or more.
  • ☀️ HF: "the higher the sun, the higher the frequency".

Contingencies (current procedure)

🔁 Unable to keep the clearance Turn at least 30° left or right to join a track 5 NM off the cleared one, then climb or descend so the level differs by 500 ft from normal levels. Broadcast on 121.5 MHz (or 123.45) and keep a good lookout.
  • ⛈️ Weather deviation: under 5 NM, stay level; beyond 5 NM, change level by 300 ft.
  • ↘️ Strategic lateral offset: centre line or up to 2 NM right only.
  • 📏 Report a height deviation of more than 300 ft.
📝 Keep in mind Older procedures used a 45° turn and a 15 NM offset. The current figures are 30° and 5 NM.

10 Special Procedures and Hazards Chapter 10

Ice on the airframe, birds, and the noise a departing aeroplane makes: each has its own procedure.

🎯 How ice forms🎯 Frost🎯 Effect of ice🎯 NADP 1 and 2🎯 Noise runway limits
💡 Key idea

Ice needs liquid water and a surface below 0 °C. A thin deposit can cut lift by up to 30% and raise drag by up to 40%, so take-off starts only with clean surfaces.

Noise abatement departures

NADP 1 — close in

Reduce power at or above 800 ft, climb at V2 + 10–20 kt with take-off flap to 3000 ft, then accelerate.

NADP 2 — distant

From 800 ft, accelerate and retract flap, then climb at zero-flap speed + 10–20 kt to 3000 ft.

Never below 800 ft. Turns not below 500 ft, bank limited to 15°.

When noise must not choose the runway

  • 💨 Crosswind over 15 kt or tailwind over 5 kt (including gusts).
  • ☁️ Ceiling below 500 ft, or visibility below 1900 m.
  • ❄️ Runway not clear and dry, or windshear reported.

On approach: landing configuration by the outer marker or 5 NM, and never above the glide path. A continuous descent final approach uses about 300 ft per NM.

11 Fire and Smoke Chapter 11

Engine fires, cabin fires, smoke and hot brakes: drills, extinguishers and the classes of cargo compartment.

🎯 Engine fire drills🎯 Hand-held extinguishers🎯 Fire classes🎯 Smoke🎯 Cargo compartments🎯 Hot brakes
💡 Key idea

Human life comes first. On the ground, summon the fire service and be ready to evacuate; in the air, cut the fuel, fire the bottles and get on the ground.

  • 🔥 Turbojet drill: thrust lever closed, HP cock off, pull the fire switch (isolates fuel), fire one bottle, wait 30 seconds, then the second. Turboprop adds feathering.
  • 🧯 Flight deck: at least one Halon 1211 (or equivalent). No dry powder — poor visibility and residue.
  • 🔢 Cabin extinguishers: 7–30 seats 1 · 31–60 2 · 61–200 3 · 201–300 4 · 301–400 5 · and so on.
  • 🅰️ Class A fires: solids (paper, fabric). 🅱️ Class B: flammable liquids.

Cargo compartments

Hot brakes

  • 🛑 Rejected take-off at high mass is the classic cause; brakes peak up to 30 minutes later.
  • 💥 Tyres may ignite or burst; approach the wheel from the front or rear, never the side.
  • ⏳ Wait the brake cooling time before the next take-off.

12 Pressurisation Failure Chapter 12

When the cabin loses pressure, the time of useful consciousness is short. Masks first, then down.

🎯 Slow and rapid decompression🎯 Cabin altitude warning🎯 Emergency descent🎯 Oxygen supply
💡 Key idea

Oxygen masks on, then descend to 10 000 ft or the lowest safe level, whichever is higher. The cabin altitude warning sounds at about 10 000 ft.

Slow decompression

Gradual, may be noticed only by the warning or by symptoms of hypoxia.

Rapid decompression

Bang, mist, flying debris, cold, ear pain. Seconds count.

Crew oxygen: 2 hours if certificated above 25 000 ft (a 10-minute descent to 10 000 ft, then 110 minutes); 30 minutes otherwise. The 10-minute minimum for all passengers is a descent from maximum altitude to 15 000 ft.

13 Windshear and Microburst Chapter 13

A sudden change in wind speed or direction near the ground can take away more performance than the aeroplane has.

🎯 What windshear is🎯 Microburst sequence🎯 Recovery🎯 Signs to watch
💡 Key idea

A microburst first gives a headwind, then a downdraught, then a tailwind. The early gain in airspeed is a trap — the loss that follows is bigger.

  • 🚀 Recovery: full power briskly, pitch up to about 15° (or as the manual says), keep the configuration, follow the stick shaker limit.
  • ⬇️ Below 1000 ft a downdraught is the bigger danger.
  • 🌧️ Warning signs: virga, heavy rain from convective cloud, dust rings, reported airspeed changes.
  • 🛑 If windshear is reported or forecast, delay the take-off or approach.

14 Wake Turbulence Chapter 14

Every wing leaves two vortices behind it, strongest when the aeroplane is heavy, clean and slow.

🎯 How vortices move🎯 Categories🎯 Approach spacing🎯 Departure times

15 Security Chapter 15

Preventing and handling acts of unlawful interference, from screening on the ground to the locked flight deck door.

🎯 Unlawful interference🎯 Squawk 7500🎯 Level offset🎯 Flight deck door
💡 Key idea

Squawk 7500 (7700 if the situation warrants), tell ATC what you can, and, if you must leave the cleared route, fly at a level 500 ft off the normal IFR levels where 1000 ft separation applies.

  • 🚪 More than 60 seats or MTOM over 45 500 kg: a flight deck door lockable from either pilot seat, with a way to see who is outside.
  • 🔍 Aircraft search checklist on board; crew trained in security procedures.
  • 📝 Report acts of unlawful interference to the local authority and the operator's authority.

16 Emergency and Precautionary Landings Chapter 16

Landing off a runway, on purpose. Planning, briefing and getting everybody out.

🎯 Ditching🎯 Swell and wind🎯 Brace position🎯 90-second evacuation
💡 Key idea

Most ditchings are survivable — about 88% end with few if any injuries. The deaths come afterwards, mostly from drowning, so life jackets, rafts and a good brief matter most.

Swell

Long waves from distant weather. Land along the swell, on its back.

Wind waves

Short waves from the local wind. Into wind when the swell is small.

  • 👥 More than 44 seats: evacuation demonstrated in under 90 seconds.
  • 🦺 Life jackets are inflated outside the aircraft, never inside.
  • 📣 Brief passengers on the brace position, exits and life jackets as early as possible.
Practise: 16 Emergency and Precautionary Landings

17 Fuel Jettison Chapter 17

Dumping fuel to get down to landing mass quickly and safely.

🎯 When a system is required🎯 15 minutes🎯 6000 ft🎯 Telling ATC
💡 Key idea

A jettison system is required when the aeroplane could not otherwise get down to landing mass after a short flight, and it must dump enough in 15 minutes to meet the climb requirements.

  • ⬆️ Jettison at 6000 ft or above so the fuel evaporates before reaching the ground.
  • 📻 Tell ATC; keep clear of other traffic and of populated areas where possible.

18 Transport of Dangerous Goods by Air Chapter 18

Goods that could harm people, the aeroplane or property may fly only when the operator is approved and the goods are classified, packed, labelled and declared.

🎯 Approval on the AOC🎯 Accident versus incident🎯 Shipper's duty🎯 Notification to captain🎯 72-hour report🎯 Labels
💡 Key idea

The shipper classifies, packs and declares; the operator accepts, loads and informs. The commander gets written information on what is on board and where.

  • 📄 Two copies of the transport document, in English (plus any other language required).
  • 🔍 Inspect packages for leaks or damage before loading and on unloading; remove a leaking one and check the rest.
  • ✈️ "Cargo Aircraft Only" goods never go on a passenger aircraft.
  • 📣 In an emergency tell ATC that dangerous goods are on board; report incidents within 72 hours, including undeclared goods found in baggage.

19 Contaminated Runways Chapter 19

Water, slush, snow and ice change the braking, the drag and the distances. The words used to describe a runway carry exact meanings.

🎯 Dry, damp, wet, contaminated🎯 Depth limits🎯 Aquaplaning speed🎯 Braking action🎯 Runway condition reports
💡 Key idea

Contaminated means more than 25% of the surface covered by water or slush deeper than 3 mm, snow, or ice.

Dry

Neither wet nor contaminated (includes well-kept grooved surfaces).

Damp

Not dry, but not shiny.

Wet

Reflective, without significant standing water.

Contaminated

Over 25% covered by a contaminant.

  • 🚫 No take-off with more than 15 mm of water, slush or wet snow, or 60 mm of dry snow.
  • 💧 Aquaplaning: 9 √P knots for a rotating tyre, 7.7 √P for a non-rotating one (P in psi; 1 bar ≈ 14.5 psi).
  • 📊 Runway condition is now reported for each third of the runway with a code from 6 (dry) to 0.
  • 🗓️ A snow report (SNOWTAM) in the current format is valid for at most 8 hours.
Worked example — aquaplaning

Tyre pressure 196 psi. √196 = 14. Rotating tyre: 9 × 14 = 126 kt. Non-rotating tyre at touchdown: 7.7 × 14 ≈ 108 kt.
