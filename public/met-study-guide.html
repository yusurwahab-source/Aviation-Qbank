🌦️ Meteorology

Weather is the one part of every flight nobody can fix on the ground. This guide works from the physics up — pressure, temperature and moisture — to the clouds, winds, storms and fog they produce, and then to the codes and charts you will read before and during every flight. Each section gives the mechanism first and the practical rule second, so that you can work an answer out rather than recall it.

🌍 The Atmosphere 61 questions

A thin shell of mixed gases, held down by gravity and heated mostly from below. Nearly all the weather lives in its lowest layer.

🎯 What the air is made of🎯 The layers and their boundaries🎯 Tropopause height by latitude🎯 ISA and ISA deviation
💡 Key idea

The proportions stay the same; the amount does not. Nitrogen (~78%), oxygen (~21%) and argon (just under 1%) stay in the same ratio all the way through the troposphere, because the air keeps being stirred. What falls with height is the total pressure, and with it the partial pressure of oxygen. Water vapour is the small, variable ingredient that matters most for weather, because it changes state and carries latent heat.

The ISA temperature profile. In the real atmosphere the tropopause is higher and colder over the Equator and lower and warmer over the poles.
LayerTemperature with heightWorth remembering
TroposphereNormally fallsAbout ¾ of the mass of the atmosphere, almost all the water vapour and weather
TropopauseStops falling~16–18 km and −75 to −80°C at the Equator; ~11 km in mid-latitudes; ~8 km at the poles. Higher in summer. Breaks near 40° and 60°, where the jet streams sit
StratosphereConstant, then risingOzone absorbs ultraviolet; very stable and dry, so very little weather. Ends at the stratopause, ~50 km
Mesosphere / thermosphereFalls, then risesAbove anything an aeroplane reaches

The International Standard Atmosphere

ISA is an agreed, imaginary atmosphere used to calibrate altimeters and to compare aircraft performance. At MSL it is +15°C, 1013.25 hPa and 1.225 kg/m³. Temperature falls 1.98°C per 1000 ft (call it 2°C) to −56.5°C at 36 090 ft, stays constant to 20 km, then rises slowly. Useful pressure levels: 850 hPa ≈ 5000 ft, 700 hPa ≈ 10 000 ft, 500 hPa ≈ 18 000 ft, 300 hPa ≈ FL300, 200 hPa ≈ FL390.

Worked example — ISA deviation

At FL180 the outside air temperature is −27°C. ISA at FL180 is 15 − (2 × 18) = −21°C. The air is 6°C colder than standard, so the deviation is ISA −6. Above 36 000 ft, compare with −56.5°C rather than continuing the lapse rate.

📐 Height per hectopascalH (ft) = 96 T / P, with T in kelvin and P in hPa. At MSL in ISA that gives about 27 ft per hPa; at 300 hPa it is about 70 ft. The thinner the air, the more height for each hectopascal, and a warm column gives more height per hPa than a cold one.
✈️ Pilot tip Above about 50 000 ft, ozone and cosmic radiation become operational issues. During a strong solar flare, the practical answer is to plan a lower level.

🔽 Pressure 44 questions

Pressure is the weight of the air column above you. Everything about altimeter settings follows from how it is measured and then corrected to a reference level.

🎯 Units: hPa, mb, inHg, mmHg🎯 Warm and cold columns🎯 QFE, QNH, QFF🎯 Diurnal variation
💡 Key idea

Pressure falls more slowly with height in warm air. A warm column is "taller", so at any given height above the surface the pressure in it is higher than in a cold column that started with the same surface pressure. That one fact drives the thermal wind, the jet stream and the cold-temperature altimeter error.

SettingWhat it isAltimeter on the ground reads
QFEActual pressure at aerodrome levelZero (height above the aerodrome)
QNHQFE reduced to MSL using ISA; reported rounded down to a whole hPaAerodrome elevation
QFFQFE reduced to MSL using the actual temperature; used to draw isobars on charts— (not an altimeter setting)
1013Standard settingPressure altitude, used for flight levels
⚖️ QNH or QFF — which is bigger? They differ only in the temperature assumed for the imaginary column between the aerodrome and MSL. Above MSL and warmer than ISA: the real column is less dense, so QFF < QNH. Above MSL and colder than ISA: QFF > QNH. Below MSL, both answers flip. At an aerodrome exactly at MSL, all three are equal.
  • 1013.25 hPa = 1013.25 mb = 29.92 inHg = 760 mmHg = 101 325 Pa.
  • Surface pressure normally ranges from about 950 to 1050 hPa. The lowest values occur in the eyes of tropical cyclones, the highest in Siberian winter highs.
  • The diurnal "pressure wave" has two highs (~1000 and 2200 local) and two lows. It is about 1 hPa in temperate latitudes and up to 3 hPa in the tropics, where it can hide a weather-related change.
  • A steady fall (a "falling glass") of several hPa in three hours means an approaching or deepening low.
Worked example — QFE to QNH

Elevation 900 ft, QFE 985 hPa, 30 ft per hPa: 900 ÷ 30 = 30 hPa, so QNH ≈ 1015 hPa. Going the other way, subtract.

⚖️ Density 30 questions

Wings and engines respond to air density, not to altitude. Pressure raises density, while heat and moisture lower it.

🎯 Density vs pressure and temperature🎯 Humid air is lighter🎯 Density altitude🎯 "Hot and high"
💡 Key idea

ρ ∝ P / T. Density rises with pressure and falls with absolute temperature. Water vapour (molecular mass 18) is lighter than nitrogen and oxygen, so moist air is less dense than dry air at the same pressure and temperature. As a rule of thumb, density changes by 1% for every 3°C or every 10 hPa.

📉 With height
Falls, because the fall in pressure outweighs the fall in temperature. About 74% of the sea-level value at 10 000 ft, 50% at 20 000 ft and 25% at 40 000 ft.
🧭 With latitude
At the surface, higher towards the poles (colder air). The two are about equal near 26 000 ft. Higher up, polar air is less dense. The biggest difference is near 50 000 ft.
🛫 Performance
Low density means a longer take-off run, a lower maximum mass and a higher TAS for the same IAS. Denver, Nairobi and Sana'a are classic examples, and so are hot, humid Singapore and Bahrain.
📐 Density altitude
The ISA altitude with the same density as the actual air. It is higher than elevation whenever the air is warmer than ISA.
✈️ Pilot tip High, hot and humid is the worst combination. A cold, high-pressure winter morning gives better-than-book performance.

🌀 Pressure Systems 47 questions

Lows, highs, troughs, ridges and cols. Each has its own vertical motion, and that vertical motion decides the weather.

🎯 Buys Ballot's law🎯 Convergence and divergence🎯 Typical weather of each🎯 Drift and altimeter
💡 Key idea

Low: air converges at the surface and rises, which gives cloud and rain. High: air sinks and diverges at the surface, which gives fine weather under an inversion. In the Northern Hemisphere, stand with your back to the wind and the low is on your left (on your right in the Southern Hemisphere). Around a low the wind blows anticlockwise and slightly inwards; around a high, clockwise and slightly outwards.

SystemChart shapeTypical weather
DepressionClosed isobars, lowest at the centreExtensive cloud and rain; strong winds where isobars are tight; good visibility outside rain. Polar front lows move west to east and last about 10–14 days
TroughV-shaped isobars out from a lowA line of enhanced cloud and showers, often a front
AnticycloneClosed, widely spaced isobars, highest at the centreSummer: clear, light winds, haze. Winter: low stratus or fog trapped under the subsidence inversion. Slow-moving, sometimes blocking for weeks
RidgeRounded isobars out from a highA short fair spell between two lows, often lasting less than a day
ColFlat pressure between two highs and two lowsLight, variable winds. Fog in autumn and winter; thunderstorms over land in summer
  • Deepening means the central pressure of a low is falling; filling means it is rising. A high whose pressure is falling is weakening.
  • Cold highs sit over the poles and winter continents. Warm highs, such as the Azores high, come from sinking air in the subtropical part of the Hadley–Ferrel–Polar three-cell circulation.
  • Thermal lows form over hot land in summer, and over relatively warm sea in cold polar air in winter.
⬇️ Drift tells you about pressure In the Northern Hemisphere, drifting right means the wind is from the left, so low pressure lies ahead and to the right and you are flying towards it. On a fixed subscale setting the altimeter will over-read, so you are lower than you think.

🌡️ Temperature 50 questions

The sun heats the ground, and the ground heats the air. That indirect route explains lapse rates, inversions and the daily temperature curve.

🎯 Radiation, conduction, convection🎯 Lapse rates and inversions🎯 Diurnal range🎯 Land vs sea
💡 Key idea

The atmosphere is heated from below. Short-wave solar radiation passes through the air largely unabsorbed and warms the surface. The surface re-radiates long-wave energy, which water vapour and CO₂ absorb. Heat then moves upwards by conduction, convection, turbulence and the release of latent heat. Air itself conducts heat poorly.

TermMeaning
Environmental lapse rate (ELR)The actual fall of temperature with height at a place and time. On average about 2°C per 1000 ft, but it varies
InversionTemperature rising with height. Very stable; traps haze, fog and pollution. Formed by night radiation, subsidence, fronts or warm air flowing over a cold surface
Isothermal layerTemperature constant with height, as in the lower stratosphere

What makes the daily range big or small

Large range

Clear skies, light winds, dry air, inland, over bare rock or sand, in the tropics or deserts. Maximum about 1500 local time; minimum about half an hour after sunrise.

Small range

Cloud cover (it blocks heating by day and traps heat at night), strong wind (mixing), high humidity, and over the sea. The sea's daily range is tiny, under about 1°C, because water has a high specific heat and mixes and absorbs heat through depth.

  • Thermometers are kept in a ventilated screen about 1.2 m above short grass, out of direct sun.
  • Snow reflects most sunlight, while dark soil and forest absorb it. Wet ground warms more slowly than dry.
  • Kelvin = °C + 273. To convert, °F = °C × 9/5 + 32.

💧 Humidity 43 questions

Water vapour is invisible fuel. Every change of state takes in or gives out latent heat, and that heat powers clouds and storms.

🎯 Latent heat directions🎯 Relative humidity vs dew point🎯 Saturation over ice🎯 Daily cycle of RH
💡 Key idea

Warm air can hold more water vapour. The saturation vapour content roughly doubles for every 10°C rise. Relative humidity is the ratio of what the air holds to what it could hold at that temperature, so RH rises as air cools even though no water is added. The dew point is the temperature at which the air would become saturated if cooled at constant pressure; the smaller the spread between temperature and dew point, the closer the air is to cloud or fog.

Change of stateNameLatent heat
Liquid → vapourEvaporationAbsorbed (cools the surroundings)
Vapour → liquidCondensationReleased (warms the air, which drives cloud growth)
Solid → liquid / liquid → solidMelting / freezingAbsorbed / released
Vapour ↔ solid directlySublimation / depositionAbsorbed / released (the largest amounts)
  • Air saturated with respect to water is supersaturated with respect to ice, so ice crystals grow at the expense of supercooled droplets. This is the start of most precipitation.
  • Humidity is measured with a wet- and dry-bulb psychrometer. The wet bulb is cooled by evaporation, and the bigger the difference between the two readings, the drier the air.
  • Over a day, absolute humidity changes little, but RH is highest around dawn (coldest) and lowest in mid-afternoon.
  • Humidity mixing ratio is the grams of vapour per kilogram of dry air. It stays constant in a rising parcel until saturation.

📈 Adiabatics & Stability 57 questions

Lift a parcel of air and compare its temperature with the air around it. Whether it keeps rising or sinks back decides whether you get towering cumulus or flat, layered cloud.

🎯 DALR and SALR🎯 Absolute and conditional instability🎯 Föhn sums🎯 How stability changes
💡 Key idea

A rising parcel cools by expansion with no heat exchange (adiabatically): 3°C per 1000 ft while dry (DALR), and less once saturated (SALR), because condensation releases latent heat: about 1.8°C per 1000 ft on average in temperate latitudes, nearer 1.5°C in warm, moist low-level air. The SALR is smallest in warm air and nears the DALR high up and at high latitudes, where there is little moisture to condense. Compare the parcel with the environmental lapse rate (ELR):

ELR compared with the adiabatic ratesStabilityTypical result
ELR < SALR (including inversions and isothermal layers)Absolutely stableLayer cloud, poor visibility in haze, smooth air
SALR < ELR < DALRConditionally unstable: stable if dry, unstable once saturatedCumulus that can grow tall once condensation starts
ELR > DALRAbsolutely unstable (usually only near a heated surface)Thermals, gusts, showers, good visibility outside them
ELR = DALR or SALRNeutral (dry or saturated)The parcel stays where it is put
Föhn: moisture lost as rain on the windward side means the descending air warms at the dry rate for longer than it cooled. It arrives warmer and drier than it started.
Worked example — Föhn temperature

Air at +20°C, dew point +12°C, at sea level crosses a 10 000 ft ridge and descends to sea level. Cloud base = (20 − 12) × 400 = 3200 ft. To the base it cools at 3°C/1000 ft: 20 − 9.6 = 10.4°C. From 3200 to 10 000 ft (6800 ft) it cools at an SALR of, say, 1.5°C/1000 ft: 10.4 − 10.2 = 0.2°C at the summit. It then descends 10 000 ft dry, warming by 30°C, to about +30°C on the lee side, 10°C warmer than it started.

What changes stability

Becomes more unstable

Heating from below (a sunny afternoon, cold air over warm sea), cooling aloft, adding moisture low down, or lifting a whole layer that is moist at the bottom and dry at the top.

Becomes more stable

Cooling from below (clear nights, warm air over cold sea), warming aloft, subsidence under a high, which builds an inversion.

🌪️ Turbulence & Windshear 55 questions

Bumps come from three places: the friction layer near the ground, in and around cloud, and in clear air near jet streams and mountains. Windshear close to the ground is the one that can actually put an aircraft on the ground.

🎯 Thermal vs mechanical🎯 Mountain waves and rotors🎯 CAT near jets🎯 Microburst sequence
💡 Key idea

Windshear is any change of wind velocity over a short distance, vertical currents included. Vertical shear is quoted in knots per 100 ft, horizontal shear in knots per 1000 ft. Turbulence is what an aircraft feels when it flies through shear that changes on the scale of its own size.

☀️ Thermal turbulence
Convection over land heated by the sun. Worst around 1500 local on clear days; almost absent over the open sea.
🏢 Mechanical turbulence
Wind over rough ground, hills, buildings and hangars near a threshold. Grows with wind speed and roughness. The friction layer is about 2000–3000 ft deep by day and shallower at night.
🏔️ Mountain waves
Need a strong wind within about 30° of perpendicular to the ridge, increasing with height, and a stable layer near ridge top. Lenticular clouds sit on the wave crests; the rotor under the first crest is the worst place to be. Waves can exist in dry air with no cloud at all.
🌬️ Clear air turbulence
Strongest on the cold (polar) side of a jet, just below the core, where shear is greatest. Worse in sharply curved troughs, in developing or fast-moving jets, and over mountains.

Reporting intensity

IntensityWhat it feels like
LightSlight, erratic changes in attitude or altitude; seat belts felt slightly
ModerateChanges in attitude or altitude, but always under control; about 0.5–1.0 g changes at the CG; unsecured objects move
SevereLarge, abrupt changes; aircraft may be momentarily out of control; occupants forced against the belts
ChopRapid, rhythmic bumps with little change of altitude or attitude

"Occasional" means less than a third of the time, "intermittent" between a third and two thirds, and "continuous" more than two thirds.

A microburst lasts only a few minutes. The initial headwind tempts a crew to reduce power just before the downdraught and tailwind arrive, which is the dangerous part.
✈️ Pilot tip In mountain waves, cross the ridge at right angles, at turbulence penetration speed, with plenty of height. A useful guide is clearance at least equal to the ridge's height above the surrounding terrain. At high altitude, reduce to penetration speed and consider descending to widen the buffet margin.

📏 Altimetry 63 questions

An altimeter is a barometer with a scale in feet, calibrated for an atmosphere that never quite exists. Every error comes from the real air not matching ISA.

🎯 High to low, look out below🎯 Transition altitude and level🎯 27 ft per hPa sums🎯 Cold-temperature error
💡 Key idea

Flying towards lower pressure or colder air at a constant indicated altitude, the aircraft descends. Both errors make the altimeter over-read, so true altitude is below indicated altitude. That is why regional QNH is the lowest forecast value, why settings are rounded down, and why minimum altitudes are increased in very cold weather.

TermDefinition
AltitudeVertical distance above MSL (QNH set)
HeightVertical distance above a specified datum, such as the aerodrome (QFE set)
ElevationHeight above MSL of a point on, or fixed to, the ground
Flight levelA surface of constant pressure referenced to 1013.25 hPa, in hundreds of feet
Transition altitudeAt or below it, vertical position is an altitude on QNH
Transition levelThe lowest usable flight level above the transition altitude
Transition layerThe airspace between the two
Worked example — wrong setting

Aerodrome elevation 300 ft, 1020 hPa set but the actual QNH is 1005 hPa. The setting is 15 hPa too high, and each hPa is worth 27 ft, so the altimeter reads 15 × 27 = 405 ft too high: 705 ft. Setting a higher number on the subscale always makes the altimeter read higher.

Worked example — temperature error

Indicating 3000 ft on QFE with the air below at ISA −20. The correction is 4 ft × 20 × 3 = 240 ft, so true height ≈ 2760 ft. The error is proportional to the ISA deviation and to the height above the setting datum. It is zero on the ground at the datum and largest at the start of an approach.

🧮 Safe flight levels To find the lowest usable flight level for a safety altitude, correct the safety altitude to pressure altitude using the lowest forecast QNH (QNH below 1013 means add 27 ft per hPa), then allow for temperature below ISA, and round up to the next available level.
  • Pressure altitude = elevation + (1013 − QNH) × 27 ft.
  • Density altitude ≈ pressure altitude + 120 ft per °C above ISA.
  • Two aircraft at the same flight level stay separated even in different temperatures: they both fly the same pressure surface, whatever their true altitudes.

🧭 Winds 71 questions

Air starts to move from high to low pressure; the Earth's rotation turns it until it blows along the isobars; friction near the ground turns it back towards the low.

🎯 Pressure gradient + Coriolis🎯 Geostrophic and gradient wind🎯 Surface wind rules🎯 Sea, land, katabatic, anabatic
💡 Key idea

Above the friction layer the wind is geostrophic: the pressure gradient force (towards low) is balanced by the Coriolis force (to the right of the motion in the north, to the left in the south). The wind then blows parallel to straight isobars. Coriolis = 2Ωρ V sin(latitude): zero at the Equator, greatest at the poles, and negligible within about 15° of the Equator. So the same isobar spacing gives a stronger wind at low latitude and in thinner air.

WindBalanceNote
GeostrophicPGF = CoriolisStraight, parallel isobars; above the friction layer; not near the Equator
GradientPGF, Coriolis and centrifugalCurved isobars. For the same spacing it is slower around a low and faster around a high than the geostrophic value
AntitripticPGF and frictionVery low latitudes, where Coriolis is negligible

Diurnal variation over land

By day

Convection mixes faster air down from above, so the surface wind veers and strengthens, reaching a maximum near 1500 local. The 1500 ft wind becomes lighter and backed.

By night

A surface inversion decouples the layers. The surface wind backs and drops (helping fog form), while the wind just above the inversion veers and strengthens towards the gradient wind, which gives shear at the inversion.

🏖️ Sea breeze
Land heats faster than sea on sunny days with a slack gradient. About 10 kt, reaching 8–15 nm inland; veers through the day (north). A line of cumulus marks its front, and it can carry sea fog inland.
🌙 Land breeze
The reverse at night: about 5 kt offshore, within about 5 nm of the coast.
⛷️ Katabatic
Cold, dense air sliding down slopes on clear nights, strongest over snow. Brings frost, fog and inversions to valley floors.
🌄 Anabatic
A light wind (~5 kt) up a sun-heated slope, usually weaker than a katabatic wind because gravity works against it.
  • Wind direction is always where the wind blows from: true in reports and forecasts, magnetic from ATC for take-off and landing.
  • A veer is a clockwise change in direction and a back is anticlockwise, in both hemispheres.
  • A gust is reported when it is 10 kt or more above the mean. A squall lasts a minute or more. Gale: a mean above 33 kt or gusts above 42 kt; hurricane force: above 63 kt.
  • Surface wind is measured at 10 m. 10 m/s ≈ 20 kt.

🛫 Upper Winds & Jet Streams 53 questions

Upper winds follow temperature. Because the poles are colder than the tropics, the winds aloft are mostly westerly, and where the temperature contrast is sharpest they form jet streams.

🎯 Contour charts🎯 Thermal wind🎯 Jet types and locations🎯 CAT and crossing a jet
💡 Key idea

The thermal wind is the vector difference between the winds at two levels. It blows parallel to the mean isotherms (thickness lines) of the layer between them, with cold air on the left in the north. Stand with your back to the upper wind and low pressure, low temperature and low contour heights are all on your left. A wind that veers with height means warm advection; one that backs with height means cold advection.

Chart≈ Flight level

A contour chart shows the height of one pressure surface. Contours (isohypses) behave like isobars, so the wind blows along them and is stronger where they are close together. Isotachs join points of equal wind speed. High 1000–500 hPa thickness means a warm column.

Jet streams

A jet stream is a narrow, fast current, at least 60 kt, typically about 2000 nm long, 200 nm wide and 2 nm deep. Its core lies just below the tropopause, near a break in it. Speeds above 300 kt have been measured.

JetWhere and when

Polar night jetWinter, high in the stratosphere (~50 hPa), in high latitudes

✈️ Pilot tip In the Northern Hemisphere, looking downstream along a westerly jet, the cold air is on the left. Rapid temperature change, changing drift and growing wind speed say you are near the core. If the jet is turbulent, change level or move laterally away from the core, especially out of the cold side below it, rather than staying in it.

☁️ Clouds 51 questions

Ten cloud types in three families and three height bands. Learn what each looks like and what it does to an aeroplane.

🎯 Oktas and ceiling🎯 Low, medium, high bands🎯 Which cloud gives what🎯 Warning clouds
💡 Key idea

The shape of a cloud is a picture of the stability that made it. Flat, layered (stratiform) cloud means stable air lifted gently. Heaped (cumuliform) cloud means unstable air rising in convection. Fibrous (cirriform) cloud is ice, high up. "Nimbo-" or "-nimbus" means the cloud is producing precipitation.

The ceiling is the height above aerodrome level of the lowest BKN or OVC layer. Report bases are above aerodrome level, so a base of 1200 ft at an aerodrome 600 ft high is 1800 ft AMSL. Cloud bases are measured by laser ceilometer; tops are estimated from soundings and pilot reports.

  • Cumulus grows from humilis → mediocris → congestus to cumulonimbus, first calvus (smooth top) and then capillatus (fibrous anvil top).
  • The anvil forms where the rising air meets the stable tropopause. Mamma under the anvil means strong sinking air and turbulence.
  • Fair-weather cumulus: bumpy below the base, usually smooth above the tops. Cumulus bases rise as the day warms.
  • Contrails form when exhaust water vapour condenses and freezes in cold, humid air aloft.

🌧️ Cloud Formation & Precipitation 48 questions

Cloud needs air cooled to its dew point, almost always by lifting. Precipitation needs the tiny cloud droplets to grow a million times bigger.

🎯 Five ways to lift air🎯 Bergeron vs coalescence🎯 Showers vs continuous🎯 Freezing rain
💡 Key idea

Air is lifted in five ways: turbulence (mixing up to the top of the friction layer, giving St or Sc under an inversion), orographic uplift, convection, frontal ascent, and convergence in lows and troughs. Stable air lifted gives layer cloud; unstable air lifted gives heap cloud.

Bergeron (ice crystal) process

Cloud that reaches below about 0°C has supercooled droplets and ice crystals together. The air is saturated over water but supersaturated over ice, so crystals grow at the droplets' expense until they fall. This is the main mechanism in mid-latitudes.

Coalescence

Cloud that stays warmer than 0°C, as in the tropics and summer. It needs a range of droplet sizes, so that big drops fall faster, collide with small ones and grow.

Showers come from heap cloud and are short. Continuous precipitation comes from layer cloud (classically nimbostratus) without a break for at least an hour. Intermittent precipitation comes from layer cloud and stops and starts without the sky clearing.

⛈️ Thunderstorms 59 questions

The most violent weather a pilot routinely meets, packed into one cloud: every hazard in this guide at once.

🎯 Three ingredients🎯 Air-mass vs frontal🎯 Three stages🎯 Avoidance distances
💡 Key idea

A thunderstorm needs three things: a deep layer of unstable air reaching well above the freezing level (about 10 000 ft deep or more), plenty of moisture, and a trigger: surface heating, high ground, a front or convergence. Subsidence in an anticyclone suppresses them.

  1. Cumulus (initial) stage: updraughts throughout the cell, and no precipitation reaching the ground yet.
  2. Mature stage: precipitation starts to fall from the base and drags down cold downdraughts beside updraughts of several thousand fpm. The gust front can be felt 15 nm or more ahead. The anvil spreads at the tropopause.
  3. Dissipating stage: mainly downdraughts throughout the cell, and the precipitation dies away.

⚠️ Hazards and avoidance Turbulence in, around, above and below the cloud; hail up to about 45 000 ft and under the anvil miles from the core; severe icing between about 0°C and −45°C; lightning (most strikes within about 5000 ft of the freezing level); microbursts and windshear; compass errors and static. On radar: avoid echoes by 5 nm below FL200 (10 nm if growing) and by 20 nm above FL300. Visually, keep at least 10 nm laterally and 5000 ft vertically from a tall, growing or anvilled storm.
✈️ Pilot tip Weather radar sees water, not ice. A dry, frozen top or hail shaft can look weaker than it is, so treat a weak echo high in the storm as worse than it looks. Lightning detectors map electrical discharges instead.

🌫️ Visibility & Fog 55 questions

Fog closes more aerodromes than any other weather. Knowing which kind you face tells you when it will form and when it will clear.

🎯 Mist, fog, haze🎯 Radiation vs advection🎯 How fog clears🎯 RVR
💡 Key idea

Fog is visibility below 1000 m in water droplets; mist is 1000 m or more (usually up to 5000 m); haze is the same reduction from dry particles. Poor visibility belongs to stable air, inversions and light winds, which is why highs and cols are the foggy pressure systems.

Radiation fog

Clear night, moist air, light wind of about 2–8 kt (calm gives dew or frost; above 10–15 kt gives low stratus instead). Over land only, in autumn and winter, first in valleys, thickening until just after sunrise. Cleared by the sun heating from below or by stronger wind. In a winter high it can persist all day.

Advection fog

Warm, moist air moving over a colder surface. It can form with moderate wind, at any time of day, and over the sea. Over land in north-west Europe it is a winter feature; over the sea, late spring and early summer. It clears with a change of air mass, or turns to stratus above about 15 kt.

⛰️ Hill fog
Low cloud with its base below the hilltops.
♨️ Steaming fog
Very cold air over much warmer water: Arctic sea smoke near Greenland, Iceland and in fjords.
🌧️ Frontal fog
Rain saturating the air under a front until the base reaches the ground; a belt up to about 200 nm wide.
🥶 Freezing fog / ice fog
Supercooled droplets that rime surfaces / ice crystals in extreme cold.

Runway visual range

RVR is how far a pilot on the centreline can see the runway markings or lights. It is measured by transmissometer or forward-scatter meter at the touchdown zone, mid-point and stop end. It is reported when visibility or RVR falls below 1500 m, or with shallow fog. It is usually greater than the meteorological visibility because bright lights can be seen further away. RVR is observed, never forecast.

✈️ Pilot tip Above a haze or fog layer, the horizontal view can be excellent while the slant view down to the runway is poor, and haze is worst looking into the sun. Near a city, the visibility is better on the upwind side.

🧊 Icing 56 questions

Water can stay liquid well below 0°C, down to about −40°C. When those supercooled drops hit an aircraft, they freeze on it.

🎯 Supercooled water🎯 Clear, rime, mixed, hoar frost🎯 Freezing rain🎯 Engine icing
💡 Key idea

Airframe icing needs supercooled liquid droplets and an airframe at or below 0°C. The bigger the drops and the closer the temperature is to 0°C, the more of each drop runs back before freezing, and the worse the ice. Below about −20°C, most cloud is ice crystals, which do not stick.

TypeHow it formsCharacter
Clear (glaze)Large drops; part freezes at once and the rest flows back. Cu, Cb and Ns, 0 to about −20°C; freezing rainDense, heavy, sticks hard, distorts the aerofoil; the most dangerous

🌧️ Freezing rain Rain falls from a warm layer aloft into sub-zero air below, typically in a shallow layer ahead of a warm front or occlusion in winter. It builds clear ice very fast; even short encounters can be hazardous, and ice protection may not cope. Leave at once, for example by turning back or climbing into the warm layer.
  • Ice reduces lift (by around 30%) and increases drag (by around 40%), raises the stall speed, adds weight, and blocks pitot-static instruments.
  • In a cumulus the worst icing is in the lower part of the cloud, just above the freezing level. The thinnest parts (tailplane edges, struts, probes) collect ice first.
  • Carburettor icing needs no sub-zero air: fuel evaporation and venturi expansion can produce it from about −10°C to +25°C or more in moist air.
  • Turbine intake icing comes from snow or supercooled water, worst at high RPM and low speed. Cold-soaked fuel can frost the wings on the ground.
  • On the ground the aircraft must be free of frost, ice and snow. Holdover time is how long the de/anti-icing fluid will keep protecting.

🧊🔥 Air Masses & Fronts 57 questions

An air mass takes on the character of the surface it sat over. When it moves, that character changes from below, and where two air masses meet, a front forms.

🎯 Air mass codes🎯 The five European air masses🎯 Warm and cold front sequence🎯 Slopes and speeds
💡 Key idea

Warmed from below, an air mass becomes unstable; cooled from below, it becomes stable. Air moving over a warmer surface gets heap cloud, showers and good visibility. Air moving over a colder surface gets stratus, drizzle, fog and poor visibility. The letters in an air mass code are its moisture (m maritime / c continental), its source (A Arctic, P polar, T tropical), and sometimes whether it is colder (k) or warmer (w) than the surface beneath it.

Air mass over NW EuropeTypical weather
Arctic maritime (mA)Very unstable after a long sea track: large Cu, heavy snow showers in winter

A depression moving east. The observer to the east sees, in order, the warm-front clouds lowering and thickening, the warm sector, and then the cold front.

Warm front passes

Ahead of it: pressure falls steadily and continuous rain becomes heavier under Ns. As it passes: the wind veers, temperature and dew point rise, and the pressure fall slows. Then the warm sector: St, drizzle and poor visibility, especially in winter. A warm front moves at about ⅔ of the geostrophic wind component at right angles to it.

Cold front passes

A sharp veer, squalls, heavy showers, a sudden drop in temperature and dew point, and pressure starting to rise. Behind it: Cu and Cb, showers, and very good visibility between them. It moves at about the full perpendicular geostrophic component. Its weather is more intense but narrower than a warm front's, and more intense in winter.

  • The Polar Front separates polar and tropical air. In the North Atlantic it runs roughly from Florida to SW Britain in winter, and from Newfoundland to NW Britain in summer.
  • Depressions come in families, the next one forming on the trailing cold front. They move parallel to the warm-sector isobars.
  • The jet lies parallel to the fronts in the warm air: about 400 nm ahead of the surface warm front and about 200 nm behind the surface cold front.
  • At 10 000 ft with a 1:150 slope, the warm front surface is 1 500 000 ft (about 250 nm) ahead of its surface position.

🔗 Occlusions 35 questions

The cold front moves faster than the warm front and eventually catches it, lifting the warm sector off the ground.

🎯 Warm or cold occlusion?🎯 Where the rain falls🎯 Embedded Cb🎯 Triple point and trowal
💡 Key idea

The colder of the two cold air masses stays at the surface. If the air ahead is colder, the cold front rides up over it and you get a warm occlusion. This is the typical winter case over Western Europe, when continental air ahead is very cold. If the air behind is colder, it undercuts and you get a cold occlusion, which is more common in summer. A station that is colder ahead of the occlusion than behind it is under a warm occlusion.

Warm occlusionCold occlusion
Chart line continuesAlong the warm frontAlong the cold front
Most precipitationAhead of the surface occlusionBehind the surface occlusion
Special riskRain ice in very cold air belowConvective weather as in a cold front

The meeting point of the warm, cold and occluded fronts is the triple point (point of occlusion), a favourite place for a new secondary low, especially where mountains hold the fronts back. The lifted warm air forms a trowal (trough of warm air aloft). An occluding low slows down and curves to the left in the Northern Hemisphere; it deepens for about four days and can take ten days or more to fill. On a chart, an occlusion is drawn with triangles and semicircles on the same side of the line.

⚠️ Main hazard Embedded cumulonimbus: the cold front's convective cloud is pushed into the warm front's layer cloud, so the storms are hidden in stratiform cloud, with icing and turbulence. A slow-moving occlusion near the depression centre gives the most persistent weather.

🌀 Other Depressions 48 questions

Not every low is a polar front low. Mountains, heating, cold pools and warm tropical seas all make lows of their own.

🎯 Lee lows🎯 Thermal and polar lows🎯 Cold air pools🎯 Tropical revolving storms
⛰️ Orographic (lee) low
Air flows round the ends of a range, leaving a shortage of air in its lee. The Gulf of Genoa is the classic case as a cold front reaches the Alps. Dry air gives Föhn; moist, unstable air gives Cb and squalls.
☀️ Thermal low
Heating expands the column, so air flows out aloft and surface pressure falls. Weakens with height, possibly to a high aloft. In summer over land it brings storms; in winter it forms over warm inland seas (Mediterranean, Black, Caspian).
❄️ Polar low
Arctic air over a much warmer sea in winter: Cb and heavy snow showers. Not a frontal low.
🥶 Cold air pool
Cold air aloft, seen on thickness or contour charts but not on a surface chart. Over summer land it brings showers and thunderstorms.
➕ Secondary low
A smaller low within a bigger circulation. It may grow into the next main low, or hold up a cold front and make its timing hard to forecast.
🌪️ Tornado
Up to a few hundred metres across, with violent winds and a sudden pressure drop. A hook echo on radar. US peak in the late afternoon; European ones are smaller.

🗺️ Global Climatology 56 questions

The big picture: belts of pressure and wind that move north and south with the sun, and the monsoons that reverse with the seasons.

🎯 Pressure and wind belts🎯 The ITCZ🎯 Monsoons🎯 January vs July
💡 Key idea

From the Equator outwards in each hemisphere: the equatorial trough (ITCZ and the doldrums) → north-east / south-east trade winds → subtropical highs at about 30–35° (the "Horse Latitudes") → the westerlies at about 40–60° → the temperate lows → the polar easterlies → the polar high. Two kinds of orbit and three kinds of picture. Knowing which one you are looking at tells you what the brightness means.

Over the Equator at about 36 000 km, one orbit in 24 h. A continuous view of about a third of the globe (Meteosat at 0°: about 70°W–70°E), good for time-lapse. Distorted towards the poles.

About 850 km up, about 100 minutes per orbit, scanning a strip. Sharper detail and polar coverage, but each place is seen only about twice a day.

🌋 Volcanic ash Ash melts in the combustion section and fuses onto the turbine, so engines can flame out. It also abrades windscreens and leading edges and blocks pitot tubes. It barely shows on weather radar and is hard to see at night. Colour code: GREEN normal · YELLOW elevated unrest · ORANGE heightened unrest or eruption with little ash · RED eruption with significant ash, or imminent.

📻 Met Information in Flight 30 questions

Once airborne, the weather picture is kept current by broadcasts, data link and the reports pilots themselves send back.

VOLMET

Weather for a group of aerodromes in plain language: METARs with TREND, sometimes TAFs and SIGMETs. Regional services on VHF; long-range ones on HF at fixed times each hour (Shannon matters for the North Atlantic). Updated about every half hour. Useful for destination and alternates, though you may have to wait for the aerodrome you need.

ATIS

A continuous broadcast for one aerodrome: runway, wind (magnetic), visibility, weather, cloud, temperature, dew point, QNH, transition level and warnings. Each update gets a new letter, which you quote on first contact. Sometimes on a VOR voice channel, or separate for arrivals and departures. Even with the ATIS acknowledged, ATC still passes the QNH.

  • A special air-report is required for severe turbulence or icing, severe mountain waves, thunderstorms, volcanic ash and similar hazards. Give position, level, time, intensity and type. Automated reports by data link give routine wind and temperature data.
  • Data link gives D-ATIS and METAR/TAF on request, independent of broadcast schedules.
  • Checking METARs from aerodromes upwind of the destination shows what is on its way.
✈️ Pilot tip A destination METAR well below minima with NOSIG means no improvement is expected within two hours, so plan the diversion now rather than holding in hope.
