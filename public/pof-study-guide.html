01 Basic Aerodynamics Fundamentals

Aerodynamic force is the surface integral of pressure and shear stress. The pressure field follows from four relations — state, continuity, momentum and energy; shear stress follows from viscosity, scaled by Reynolds number.

Equation of state and the standard atmosphere 1.1

p = ρ·R·T     R = 287.05 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹
σ = ρ/ρ₀ = δ/θ     δ = p/p₀    θ = T/T₀

Continuity 1.2

ṁ = ρ·A·V = constant
dA/A = (M² − 1)·dV/V     dρ/ρ = −M²·dV/V
  • Incompressible limit: A₁V₁ = A₂V₂; velocity ∝ 1/A.

  • At M 0.3 stagnation density exceeds free-stream density by 4.6 %: the practical boundary of the incompressible approximation.

Viscosity and Reynolds number 1.9

02 Aerofoils & Lift Theory Aerofoils

A section is defined by three functions of angle of attack — cl(α), cd(α), CM(α) — set by camber line, thickness distribution and leading-edge radius. A finite wing modifies each through downwash and planform.

Pressure distribution 2.2

Shaded area between the curves = cl; its chordwise centroid = centre of pressure
  • Front stagnation point (Cp = +1) moves down and aft on the lower surface as α rises.
  • Upper-surface suction supplies about two-thirds or more of cl at moderate α.
  • A higher suction peak requires a steeper pressure recovery (dp/dx > 0) to the trailing edge; boundary-layer separation under that gradient defines the stall.
  • Symmetric section at α = 0: equal suction on both surfaces, cl = 0.
  • Load centroids: triangular (peak at LE) → c/3; uniform → c/2; flat plate, subsonic → c/4.

Section lift curve 2.3

  • Real sections: a₀ ≈ 0.10–0.11 deg⁻¹. Linear to about 0.85 cl,max; curvature follows as trailing-edge separation advances.
  • Post-stall: cl ↓, cd ↑ steeply, CM more nose-down (load moves toward mid-chord).
  • Camber shifts the curve left (α₀ more negative), leaves the slope unchanged, raises cl,max.
  • L′ = cl·q·c: cl 0.8, q 3 kPa, c 2 m → 4,800 N/m.

Aerodynamic centre and centre of pressure 2.4

Thickness and leading edge 2.6

  • t/c ↑: rLE ↑ ∝ (t/c)², cl,max ↑ to a maximum near 12–15 % then ↓, form drag ↑, Mcrit ↓.
  • Thin section (≲ 9 %): thin-aerofoil stall — leading-edge bubble lengthens, gradual loss of slope.
  • Moderate (≈ 9–15 %): leading-edge stall — short bubble bursts, abrupt lift loss.
  • Thick (≳ 15 %): trailing-edge stall — separation advances forward from the trailing edge, gradual.
  • Maximum thickness aft: minimum-pressure point aft, longer favourable gradient, longer laminar run; a low-drag bucket in a narrow cl range, outside which transition moves forward and cd rises sharply.

Reynolds-number effects 2.7

  • Re ↑: cl,max ↑, stall α ↑, cd,min ↓ (cf ∝ Re−0.5 laminar, ∝ Re−0.2 turbulent).
  • Re ≲ 5 × 10⁵: laminar separation, transition in the free shear layer, turbulent reattachment — a laminar separation bubble; its bursting produces an abrupt stall.
  • Tapered wing: tip Re = λ × root Re → lower local cl,max outboard.
  • Roughness and contamination move transition forward: cl,max and stall α fall.

04 Boundary Layer Boundary Layer

No-slip at the wall creates a thin viscous layer in which shear stress is of the same order as inertia. Its state — laminar or turbulent — sets skin friction; its momentum against the imposed pressure gradient sets separation, and therefore form drag and the stall.

Pressure gradient and separation 4.4

  • dp/dx > 0 decelerates the near-wall fluid first; H rises; the profile inflects; separation at (∂u/∂y)wall = 0, reversed flow downstream.
  • Turbulent layers carry more near-wall momentum and survive larger pressure recoveries than laminar layers.
  • Laminar separation at low Re: free shear layer transitions and may reattach — a separation bubble; bursting gives abrupt stall.
  • Rising α steepens the recovery behind the suction peak: trailing-edge separation moves forward, lift slope falls, stall follows.
  • Separated region: no pressure recovery → loss of suction (lift) and a low-pressure wake (form drag).
  • Displacement at the trailing edge reduces effective camber and circulation: real lift slopes 0.10–0.11 deg⁻¹, below 2π rad⁻¹.

08 Secondary Controls & Trim Trim

Trim sets the flight condition at which control force is zero, by adjusting a hinge moment (tab) or tailplane incidence (stabiliser). It does not change stability or add control authority. Spoilers and speed brakes modify drag and lift for descent, roll and deceleration.

Trim tabs 8.1

b₂·δe + b₃·δt = 0  ⇒  δt = −(b₂/b₃)·δe
  • The tab deflects opposite to the surface it holds: elevator up ⇔ tab down.
  • |b₂| 0.006/deg, |b₃| 0.004/deg, δe 4° up → δt 6° down.
  • Tab trim consumes elevator travel in the trimmed direction.
  • Ground-adjustable tabs correct fixed rigging asymmetries; servo-tab systems trim by offsetting the tab datum.

Trimmable stabiliser 8.2

  • Incidence changes by jackscrew (irreversible); elevator faired with full travel; lower trim drag; large trim range for flap, Mach and CG changes.
  • Nose-up trim: stabiliser leading edge down, more negative incidence, more download.
  • Take-off setting from CG: forward CG → more nose-up trim.
  • Runaway: the stabiliser outweighs the elevator; opposing authority shrinks as mistrim grows.

14 Loads & Limitations Limitations

How much load an airframe is built to take, and the speeds that protect it.

Load factor = lift ÷ weight. A level turn raises it because lift must also supply the horizontal, centripetal component. A V-n diagram bounds the envelope: the aerodynamic stall limit on one side, the structural limit load factor on the other. Manoeuvring speed (VA) is where the wing stalls exactly at limit load factor — it falls with weight, and above VA an abrupt full control input can overstress the structure before the wing stalls to protect it.

15 Windshear & Atmospheric Effects Windshear

A sudden change in wind speed or direction, and the atmospheric patterns that produce it.

A decreasing headwind (or increasing tailwind) on approach momentarily drops airspeed and lift, sinking the aircraft below path; an increasing headwind does the opposite, ballooning it above path — both because inertia keeps groundspeed from adjusting instantly. A microburst delivers the worst combination in sequence: increasing headwind, then a downdraft, then a sharply increasing tailwind, right as an aircraft is trying to climb away — which is why the standard response to significant windshear is a prompt go-around.

Mountain waves form when stable air is forced over terrain by a strong, roughly perpendicular wind, producing standing waves and severe turbulence well downwind. Clear air turbulence is most often tied to wind shear at the edges of the jet stream, with no cloud to give it away.
