01 Basic Aerodynamics Fundamentals

Everything else in this guide builds on a small set of definitions: what angle of attack actually means, how a wing produces a pressure difference, and what happens in the thin layer of air right at its surface. Get these right and the rest — lift, drag, the stall, stability — mostly falls out of them.

The four forces 1.1

In steady, level, unaccelerated flight, four forces act on the aircraft and balance in pairs along two axes: lift equals weight, and thrust equals drag. Any imbalance is what produces a climb, a descent, or a change in speed — there is no such thing as "extra" lift or thrust without one of those following.

Lift/weight and thrust/drag: two independent pairs, each balanced in steady, unaccelerated flight

Angle of attack vs pitch attitude 1.2

Angle of attack is the angle between the wing's chord line and the relative airflow — the airflow produced by the aircraft's own motion through the air, which always runs exactly opposite to the flight path. Pitch attitude is the angle between the fuselage and the horizon. The two are easy to confuse but are not the same thing: a glider in a steep descent can have a nose-down pitch attitude and still be flying at a high angle of attack, because its flight path is pointed even further down than its nose.

Bernoulli's principle and pressure 1.3

Along a streamline in subsonic, incompressible flow, the sum of static pressure and dynamic pressure stays constant. Air accelerating over the curved upper surface of a wing gains dynamic pressure, so its static pressure falls — producing a region of lower pressure above the wing than below it. That pressure difference, integrated over the whole wing area, is lift.

The airfoil 1.4

The chord line is a straight line from the leading edge to the trailing edge. Camber is the curvature of the mean line halfway between the upper and lower surfaces, measured against the chord line — a symmetrical airfoil has zero camber; most wings have some positive camber, which is part of why they can still produce lift at zero angle of attack.

The boundary layer 1.5

Air in direct contact with the wing's surface is slowed by viscosity, forming a thin boundary layer. Close to the leading edge this layer is laminar — smooth, low-friction, but with little energy near the surface, so it separates easily under an adverse pressure gradient. Further aft it typically transitions to turbulent flow, which mixes higher-energy air down toward the surface, resisting separation for longer at the cost of higher skin friction drag.

Air density 1.6

Lift is directly proportional to air density. At a constant angle of attack and true airspeed, descending into denser air (or the air simply being colder or at lower altitude) increases the mass of air the wing deflects each second, and lift increases with it — the same relationship that means an aircraft needs a higher true airspeed to produce the same lift at high density altitude.

02 Lift and Drag Lift & Drag

Lift and drag are not independent — every increase in lift carries a drag cost, and where that cost comes from changes with speed. This section is the total drag curve and everything that shapes it.

The lift equation 2.1

SymbolFactorNotes
CLLift coefficientSet by angle of attack and airfoil shape/configuration
ρAir densityFalls with altitude and rising temperature
V²True airspeed, squaredThe dominant term — double the speed, quadruple the lift at constant CL
SWing areaFixed for a given aircraft (flaps aside)

L = CL × ½ρV²S. Temperature and altitude are not separate terms — they only matter because of their effect on ρ.

Types of drag 2.2

Drag typeCauseBehaviour with speed
Induced dragA by-product of generating lift — wingtip vorticesHighest at low speed / high AoA, falls as speed rises
Skin friction dragAir viscosity acting through the boundary layerRises with speed and surface area
Form dragThe overall shape disturbing the airflowRises roughly with the square of speed
Interference dragAirflow disruption where components meet (e.g. wing/fuselage)Rises with speed

Skin friction, form and interference drag are together called parasite drag — drag that buys no lift at all — and it rises with the square of speed, the opposite trend to induced drag.

The total drag curve 2.3

Total drag is the sum of induced and parasite drag; its minimum is the point of maximum lift-to-drag ratio — the best glide speed

Where the falling induced-drag curve crosses the rising parasite-drag curve, total drag is at its minimum — and the lift-to-drag ratio is at its maximum. That single speed is the best glide speed in an unpowered descent.

Wingtip vortices and aspect ratio 2.4

Higher pressure air beneath the wing spills around the tip toward the lower pressure above it, rolling up into a trailing vortex — the physical source of induced drag. A higher aspect ratio wing (long and narrow) has proportionally less of its span affected by this tip spillage, so it produces less induced drag for the same lift; winglets work the same way, by interrupting the vortex directly rather than lengthening the span.

Ground effect 2.5

Within roughly one wingspan of the surface, the ground restricts downwash and limits vortex formation at the tips, reducing induced drag and effectively increasing lift at a given angle of attack — which is why an aircraft can "float" down the runway if it arrives over the threshold too fast.

03 Stall & High-Lift Devices The Stall

The single most important idea in this whole guide: a stall is an angle-of-attack event, not a speed event. Everything else in this section is a consequence of that one sentence.

The core rule A wing stalls when its critical angle of attack is exceeded — full stop. It can stall at any airspeed, in any attitude, at any power setting, if the wing is asked for more angle of attack than it can sustain. A "stalling speed" published for one weight, load factor and configuration is just the speed at which that angle happens to be reached under those specific conditions.

Load factor and stalling speed 3.1

Because the wing must reach the same critical angle of attack at a higher speed to support a higher load, stalling speed scales with the square root of load factor.

Load factorStall speed multiplierExample
1g1.00×Straight and level
1.41g (45° bank)1.19×Moderate level turn
2g (60° bank)1.41×Steep level turn
4g2.00×Aggressive manoeuvring

Weight and stalling speed 3.2

The same square-root relationship applies to weight: a 21% increase in weight raises stalling speed by only about 10% (the square root of 1.21), not 21% — a heavier aircraft stalls at a higher speed, but the relationship is not linear.

High-lift devices 3.3

DeviceWhere fittedMain effect
Trailing-edge flapWing trailing edgeIncreases camber and CLmax, lowering stalling speed; usually reduces the critical angle of attack slightly
Leading-edge slatWing leading edgeRe-energises the upper-surface boundary layer, raising the critical angle of attack itself
SlotFixed gap ahead of the leading edgeSame principle as a slat, without moving parts

Flaps and slats both raise CLmax and therefore lower stalling speed, but by different mechanisms — flaps mostly by adding camber, slats by delaying separation to a higher angle of attack. That distinction is a common point of confusion worth keeping straight.

Stall warning 3.4

A stall warning device — a stick shaker, an aural horn, or an angle-of-attack indexer — is set to trigger at a small margin of angle of attack before the critical angle is reached. It warns of an approaching stall; it does not, by itself, recover the aircraft from one already developed.

Icing 3.5

Even light ice roughens and distorts the leading edge, disturbing the smooth airflow needed to reach high angles of attack. The typical effect is a lower critical angle of attack and a higher stalling speed — often with reduced or no warning from a system calibrated for a clean wing.

Wing design and stall progression 3.6

Many wings use washout — a slightly lower angle of incidence at the tip than at the root — so the root reaches its critical angle of attack first. That keeps the outer wing, and the ailerons on it, flying and effective into the early stages of a stall, preserving roll control when it is needed most.

04 Stability and Control Stability & Control

Stability is about what an aircraft does on its own after a disturbance; control is about what a pilot's inputs do to it. Both are organised around the same three axes.

Static vs dynamic stability 4.1

Static stability is only about the initial tendency after a disturbance — does the aircraft's own aerodynamics push it back toward equilibrium, or away from it? Dynamic stability is about what happens next: whether that initial tendency actually converges back to equilibrium, oscillates and dies out, or oscillates and grows. An aircraft can be statically stable and still be dynamically unstable if the resulting oscillation grows over time.

Longitudinal stability and CG 4.2

CG positionEffect on longitudinal stabilityEffect on control
ForwardIncreases stability — larger stabilising moment arm to the tailplaneRequires larger elevator deflections; more resistant to a stall
Aft, toward the limitDecreases stability — closer to the neutral pointMore responsive to small elevator inputs; less margin before instability

Lateral stability: dihedral and sweep 4.3

Following a sideslip, a wing with dihedral exposes its lower, into-wind wing at a slightly higher effective angle of attack, producing more lift there and a rolling moment back toward wings-level. Sweepback contributes a similar dihedral-like effect: the forward wing in the sideslip presents more of its span perpendicular to the airflow than the trailing wing, again generating relatively more lift and a restoring roll.

Directional stability: the fin 4.4

The vertical stabilizer provides directional stability through a weathercock effect: if the aircraft yaws away from its flight path, the fin meets the resulting sideways airflow at an angle and generates a sideforce that yaws the nose back into line — the same way a weathervane aligns itself with the wind.

The phugoid 4.5

The phugoid is a slow, long-period trade between speed and height — nose up, speed falls; nose down, speed rises — repeating over tens of seconds at an almost constant angle of attack throughout. It is a different mode from the fast, short-period pitch oscillation, which involves rapid changes in angle of attack at nearly constant speed.

Primary controls 4.6

ControlAxisHow it works
ElevatorPitch (lateral axis)Changes tailplane lift/download, producing a pitching moment that changes the wing's angle of attack
AileronRoll (longitudinal axis)Differentially changes lift at each wingtip
RudderYaw (normal/vertical axis)Changes the sideforce generated by the fin

Adverse yaw 4.7

Rolling into a turn, the down-going aileron increases both lift and induced drag on its wing more than the up-going aileron reduces them on the other. That extra drag yaws the nose toward the rising wing — opposite to the intended turn — which is corrected with coordinated rudder, or designed out with differential or frise ailerons.

05 High-Speed Flight Transonic

At cruise Mach numbers, air can no longer be treated as incompressible — this section is what changes once local airflow starts approaching, and reaching, the speed of sound.

Mach number 5.1

Mach number is true airspeed divided by the local speed of sound, and the speed of sound depends only on air temperature. Because temperature falls with altitude, the same true airspeed corresponds to a higher Mach number higher up — which is why a jet's true airspeed can stay roughly the same through a climb while its Mach number keeps rising.

Critical Mach number 5.2

Airflow accelerates as it passes over the curved upper surface of a wing. The critical Mach number (MCRIT) is the freestream Mach number at which that local acceleration first reaches the speed of sound somewhere on the aircraft — even though the aircraft itself, and the air far from the wing, is still travelling below Mach 1.

Sweepback 5.3

Only the airflow component perpendicular to the leading edge governs how much the local flow accelerates. Sweeping the wing back reduces that perpendicular component for a given true airspeed, delaying the onset of local supersonic flow — raising MCRIT — which is why high-speed jet aircraft use swept wings.

Shockwaves, buffet and wave drag 5.4

Beyond MCRIT, a shockwave forms where the locally supersonic flow decelerates abruptly back to subsonic. The sharp pressure rise across it can separate the boundary layer behind it, producing Mach buffet — aerodynamically similar to a low-speed stall, but caused by compressibility, not a high angle of attack at low speed. The same shockwave introduces wave drag, seen as a steep rise in the drag curve through the transonic region.

Coffin corner 5.5

At high altitude the two buffet boundaries converge, narrowing the safe speed margin between them

As altitude increases, the low-speed buffet boundary rises in indicated airspeed (thinner air needs a higher IAS to reach the critical angle of attack) while the high-speed buffet boundary falls (a lower speed of sound and lower density both bring MCRIT closer in IAS terms). Where the two nearly meet, only a narrow speed range keeps the aircraft clear of either buffet.

Mach tuck 5.6

As shockwaves develop through the transonic region, the wing's center of pressure tends to shift aft and downwash reaching the tailplane changes, both producing an uncommanded nose-down pitching moment — Mach tuck, or tuck-under. Many high-speed aircraft counter it with a dedicated Mach trim system.
