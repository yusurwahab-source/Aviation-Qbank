01 Basic Aerodynamics Fundamentals

Everything else in this guide builds on a small set of definitions: what angle of attack actually means, how a wing produces a pressure difference, and what happens in the thin layer of air right at its surface.

The four forces 1.1

In steady, level, unaccelerated flight, four forces balance in pairs: lift equals weight, and thrust equals drag. Any imbalance produces a climb, a descent, or a change in speed.

Lift/weight and thrust/drag: two independent pairs, each balanced in steady, unaccelerated flight

Angle of attack vs pitch attitude 1.2

Angle of attack is the angle between the chord line and the relative airflow — always exactly opposite the flight path. Pitch attitude is the angle between the fuselage and the horizon. The two are easy to confuse but are not the same thing.

Two explanations of lift 1.3

Bernoulli's principle explains lift through the pressure drop that accompanies accelerated airflow over the upper surface. Newton's third law explains the same lift as the reaction to the wing deflecting a mass of air downward (downwash). Both are valid, complementary descriptions of the same physical event — and downwash tilting the local lift vector rearward is itself one explanation of induced drag.

Boundary layer basics 1.4

Close to the leading edge the boundary layer is laminar — smooth, low-friction, but easily separated. Further aft it typically transitions to turbulent flow, which resists separation for longer at the cost of more skin friction drag. Section 4 covers this in full.

Air density and pressure 1.5, 1.6

Lift is directly proportional to air density, which falls with rising temperature and altitude alike. Total (stagnation) pressure equals static plus dynamic pressure and stays constant along a streamline — the same relationship Section 11 revisits once compressibility makes it more complicated.

02 Aerofoils & Lift Theory Aerofoils

The vocabulary of an aerofoil section, and how its lift, drag and pitching moment characteristics are actually described.

Geometry 2.1

TermDefinition
Chord lineStraight line from leading edge to trailing edge
CamberCurvature of the mean camber line relative to the chord line
Thickness/chord ratioMaximum thickness as a fraction of chord — higher raises CLmax but lowers MCRIT
Leading edge radiusRounded (subsonic) vs sharp (supersonic) — affects separation onset

Center of pressure vs aerodynamic center 2.2

The center of pressure moves — forward as angle of attack rises below the stall, then sharply aft at and beyond it. The aerodynamic center (near the quarter-chord) is a fixed point about which the pitching moment coefficient stays essentially constant — the reference stability calculations actually use.

Lift curve, zero-lift angle, pitching moment 2.3

Below the stall, CL rises essentially linearly with angle of attack. A cambered section reaches zero lift only at a small negative angle of attack, and produces a nose-down pitching moment about its aerodynamic center — which is exactly why a horizontal tailplane exists.

03 Lift and Drag Lift & Drag

Lift and drag are not independent — every increase in lift carries a drag cost, and where that cost comes from changes with speed.

Types of drag 3.1

Drag typeCauseBehaviour with speed
Induced dragA by-product of lift — wingtip vortices / downwash tiltHighest at low speed, falls as speed rises
Skin frictionAir viscosity through the boundary layerRises with speed and surface area
Form dragOverall shape disturbing the airflowRises roughly with speed squared
Interference dragAirflow disruption where components meetRises with speed

The total drag curve 3.2

Total drag's minimum is the point of maximum lift-to-drag ratio — the best glide speed

Power required (thrust required × velocity) has its own minimum at a lower speed than minimum drag — the difference between best-range and best-endurance speeds. A heavier aircraft's minimum-drag speed is higher, since more speed is needed to hold the same angle of attack.

Wingtip vortices, aspect ratio, ground effect 3.3

Higher-pressure air beneath the wing spills around the tip, producing the trailing vortex that is induced drag's physical source. A higher aspect ratio or a winglet weakens this effect; flying within about one wingspan of the ground restricts it too — ground effect — which is why an aircraft can float during flare.

04 Boundary Layer Boundary Layer

The thin layer of slowed air right at the surface governs where a wing separates — and therefore stalls.

Transition and separation 4.1

The transition point is where laminar flow becomes turbulent. Separation happens when the boundary layer's kinetic energy can no longer overcome a rising (adverse) pressure gradient — and widespread upper-surface separation is, aerodynamically, a stall.

Controlling the boundary layer 4.2

DeviceEffect
Vortex generatorsMix energetic outer air into the boundary layer, delaying separation
Stall stripsDeliberately trigger early separation at the root
Blown/suction boundary layer controlRe-energises the layer to sustain a higher CLmax
Laminar flow sectionsHold laminar flow further aft to cut cruise drag, at some stall-behaviour cost

Surface contamination (frost, insects, rough paint) trips the boundary layer into turbulence — and separation — earlier than a clean wing, degrading CLmax even before ice has visibly built up.

05 Stalling The Stall

The single most important idea in this whole guide: a stall is an angle-of-attack event, not a speed event.

The core rule A wing stalls when its critical angle of attack is exceeded — at any airspeed, any attitude, any power setting. A published "stalling speed" is just the speed that angle happens to be reached at, for one weight, load factor and configuration.

Load factor and weight 5.1

Load factorStall speed multiplier
1g1.00×
1.41g (45° bank)1.19×
2g (60° bank)1.41×
4g2.00×

Stalling speed scales with the square root of load factor and the square root of weight.

Beyond the basic stall 5.2

  • Accelerated stall — stalling above 1g stalling speed due to load factor.
  • Secondary stall — caused by recovering too abruptly, before airspeed is regained.
  • Spin — autorotation once one wing is more deeply stalled than the other.
  • Deep stall — a T-tail's stabilizer blanketed by the wing's stalled wake, losing pitch-down authority.

Recovery 5.3

Reduce angle of attack first — everything else follows. A forward CG resists stalling and spinning but raises stalling speed and elevator demand; washout and stall strips make the root stall before the tip, keeping the ailerons alive.

06 High-Lift Devices High Lift

Two families of device raise CLmax by different mechanisms — which is why aircraft often carry both.

DeviceLocationMain effect
Plain / split flapTrailing edgeAdds camber; split adds more drag
Slotted flapTrailing edgeSlot re-energises boundary layer over the flap
Fowler flapTrailing edgeMoves aft as it extends — adds area and camber
Slat / slotLeading edgeRaises the critical angle of attack itself
Krueger flapLeading edgeHinged panel adding camber ahead of the wing

Flaps mostly add camber (raising CL at a given angle of attack); leading-edge devices mostly delay separation (raising the angle of attack itself). Flap asymmetry protection guards against the large, uncommandable rolling moment an uneven flap deployment would cause.

07 Flight Controls: Primary Controls

Elevator, aileron and rudder — and what happens when the airframe or airflow fights back.

ControlAxisMechanism
ElevatorPitchChanges tailplane lift/download → pitching moment → wing AoA
AileronRollDifferential lift (and drag) at each wingtip
RudderYawChanges fin/rudder sideforce

Adverse yaw and its fixes 7.1

The down-going aileron's extra induced drag yaws the nose away from the turn. Frise ailerons add drag on the up-going side by projecting below the wing; differential ailerons give more travel to the up-going aileron — both rebalance the drag.

Balance and high-speed effects 7.2

A horn (aerodynamic) balance uses airflow ahead of the hinge to assist the pilot; a mass balance moves the surface's own center of gravity forward to prevent flutter. At very high speed, aerodynamic loads can twist a flexible wing enough to cause aileron reversal; spoilers can substitute for roll control where this is a concern. Artificial feel restores a sense of load to a pilot flying fully powered controls that would otherwise transmit no natural feedback at all.

09 Longitudinal Stability Pitch

Static stability is the initial tendency after a disturbance; dynamic stability is what happens next.

CG, neutral point, static margin 9.1

Moving the CG aft shortens the tailplane's moment arm, reducing stability, until it reaches the neutral point — zero stability — beyond which the aircraft is unstable in pitch. Static margin is the CG-to-neutral-point distance, as a percentage of mean aerodynamic chord.

Phugoid vs short-period 9.2

The phugoid is a slow, tens-of-seconds trade between speed and height at constant angle of attack. The short-period mode is a fast, heavily damped oscillation in angle of attack at constant speed. A tailplane download in trimmed flight balances the wing's own nose-down pitching moment; setting the tailplane at a slightly lower incidence than the wing ("longitudinal dihedral") helps ensure the wing stalls before the tailplane does.

10 Lateral & Directional Stability Roll & Yaw

Roll stability (dihedral effect) and yaw stability (the fin) interact — and the interaction has a name.

  • Dihedral, sweep, high-wing, keel surface — all add a rolling moment following a sideslip.
  • Fin (weathercock effect) — yaws the nose back into the relative airflow.
  • Dutch roll — coupled yaw/roll, worse when lateral stability is strong relative to directional; a yaw damper corrects it automatically.
  • Spiral instability — the opposite imbalance: bank slowly, progressively increases.
  • Anhedral — deliberately reduces excess lateral stability on already-stable (usually highly swept) designs.
The trade-off Strong directional stability relative to lateral favors spiral stability but worsens Dutch roll; the reverse favors Dutch roll damping but worsens spiral stability. Real aircraft sit at a deliberate compromise.

11 High-Speed: Compressibility Mach

At cruise Mach numbers, air can no longer be treated as incompressible.

Mach number = TAS ÷ local speed of sound, which depends only on temperature. Critical Mach number (MCRIT) is the freestream Mach at which local airflow over the wing first reaches Mach 1. Sweepback raises MCRIT, because only the flow component perpendicular to the leading edge drives the local acceleration. A thicker section or a higher lift coefficient (higher weight, higher angle of attack) both lower MCRIT.

VMOStructural / control, essentially Mach-independentLower altitude

12 High-Speed: Shockwaves & Buffet Transonic

Beyond MCRIT, a shockwave forms — and everything downstream of that shockwave changes.

A normal shock decelerates local flow abruptly from supersonic to subsonic; the sharp pressure rise across it can separate the boundary layer immediately behind it, producing Mach buffet and a steep rise in drag — wave drag. The rearward shift of center of pressure as shocks form produces an uncommanded nose-down moment, Mach tuck, countered by a Mach trim system. Design responses include the area rule (smoothing the total cross-section along the fuselage) and supercritical aerofoils (a flatter top, drooped trailing edge, to weaken the shock).

Coffin corner 12.1

At high altitude the two buffet boundaries converge, narrowing the safe speed margin between them

Control surface buzz is a related, smaller effect: an oscillating shock near a hinge line repeatedly separates and reattaches the local boundary layer, vibrating the surface.

13 Propellers Propellers

Each blade is a rotating aerofoil — everything about a propeller follows from that one fact.

Left-turning tendencies 13.1

EffectCause
Torque reactionNewton's third law — airframe rolls opposite to propeller rotation
Gyroscopic precessionA pitch/yaw force appears ~90° further round the direction of rotation
Asymmetric blade effect (P-factor)Descending blade meets a higher AoA at high power/high AoA
Slipstream effectSpiralling slipstream strikes the fin asymmetrically

Constant-speed propellers 13.2

A governor coarsens blade angle when RPM rises above the selected value (loading the engine, pulling RPM back down) and fines it when RPM falls — holding a selected RPM across a wide range of speed and power, unlike a fixed-pitch propeller efficient only near one design point (its advance ratio). Feathering turns the blades edge-on after an engine failure to minimise drag and stop windmilling, which otherwise adds substantial drag and can further damage the failed engine.

Blade count and tip speed 13.3

More, narrower blades absorb more power within a limited diameter, at some efficiency cost versus fewer, longer blades. Blade tip speed can reach transonic values from rotational speed alone — the same compressibility penalties (shock, drag, noise) that affect a fast wing. High density altitude reduces both propeller thrust and engine power together.

14 Loads & Limitations Limitations

How much load an airframe is built to take, and the speeds that protect it.

Load factor = lift ÷ weight. A level turn raises it because lift must also supply the horizontal, centripetal component. A V-n diagram bounds the envelope: the aerodynamic stall limit on one side, the structural limit load factor on the other. Manoeuvring speed (VA) is where the wing stalls exactly at limit load factor — it falls with weight, and above VA an abrupt full control input can overstress the structure before the wing stalls to protect it.

TermMeaning
Limit loadMaximum expected in normal operation — no permanent deformation
Ultimate loadTypically 1.5× limit load — structure must not fail completely
VNOMax structural cruising speed — smooth air only above it
VNENever-exceed speed — an absolute limit
Wing loadingWeight ÷ wing area — higher generally means a smoother ride in gusts

15 Windshear & Atmospheric Effects Windshear

A sudden change in wind speed or direction, and the atmospheric patterns that produce it.

A decreasing headwind (or increasing tailwind) on approach momentarily drops airspeed and lift, sinking the aircraft below path; an increasing headwind does the opposite, ballooning it above path — both because inertia keeps groundspeed from adjusting instantly. A microburst delivers the worst combination in sequence: increasing headwind, then a downdraft, then a sharply increasing tailwind, right as an aircraft is trying to climb away — which is why the standard response to significant windshear is a prompt go-around.

Mountain waves form when stable air is forced over terrain by a strong, roughly perpendicular wind, producing standing waves and severe turbulence well downwind. Clear air turbulence is most often tied to wind shear at the edges of the jet stream, with no cloud to give it away.
