01 Basic Aerodynamics Fundamentals

Aerodynamic force is the surface integral of pressure and shear stress. The pressure field follows from four relations — state, continuity, momentum and energy; shear stress follows from viscosity, scaled by Reynolds number.

Equation of state and the standard atmosphere 1.1

p = ρ·R·T     R = 287.05 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹
σ = ρ/ρ₀ = δ/θ     δ = p/p₀    θ = T/T₀

Datum: p₀ = 101,325 Pa, T₀ = 288.15 K, ρ₀ = 1.225 kg/m³. Lapse rate −6.5 K/km (−1.98 K per 1,000 ft) to the tropopause at 11,000 m (36,089 ft); isothermal at 216.65 K from there to 20,000 m (65,617 ft). All temperature ratios use absolute temperature.

  • At constant p: ρ ∝ 1/T. +15 K at 288 K → ρ −5 %.
  • Troposphere: θ < 1, so σ > δ. Density falls more slowly than pressure.
  • Isothermal layer: a constant; p and ρ decay exponentially with scale height RT/g = 6,340 m, halving every ≈ 14,400 ft; σ/δ = 1/θ constant.

  • Density altitude: Hρ ≈ Hp + 120 ft/°C × (T − Tstd).

Continuity 1.2

ṁ = ρ·A·V = constant
dA/A = (M² − 1)·dV/V     dρ/ρ = −M²·dV/V
  • Incompressible limit: A₁V₁ = A₂V₂; velocity ∝ 1/A.
  • M < 1: a contraction accelerates the flow; |dV/V| = |dA/A|/(1 − M²), exceeding the incompressible value.
  • M > 1: the sign reverses; a divergent passage accelerates the flow. M = 1 occurs only at minimum area.
  • At M 0.3 stagnation density exceeds free-stream density by 4.6 %: the practical boundary of the incompressible approximation.

Force and moment coefficients 1.5

  • Fixed CL (fixed α): every reference speed ∝ √(W/S). +20 % W → +9.5 %; +44 % S → −16.7 %.
  • Level flight at constant weight: equal EAS ⇒ equal q ⇒ equal CL and α at any altitude (low M).
  • Constant TAS: CL ∝ 1/σ.
  • At constant coefficients: force ∝ V², power ∝ V³.

Equilibrium on a flight path 1.6

  • Glide at fixed α: γ and still-air distance independent of W; V and sink rate ∝ √W.

Circulation and lift 1.8

L′ = ρ·V·Γ     (Kutta–Joukowski, per unit span)
L = ṁ·Δw     αeff = α − αi     Di = L·tan αi
  • Kutta condition: of all potential-flow solutions, only one circulation places the rear stagnation point at a sharp trailing edge. That value fixes Γ.
  • Kelvin: total circulation is conserved. Bound circulation Γ is balanced by a shed starting vortex −Γ.
  • Finite span: the bound vortex turns into trailing vortices, inducing downwash w and an induced angle αi = w/V. Local lift is tilted aft by αi; its streamwise component is induced drag.
  • At constant L′ and ρ: Γ ∝ 1/V.

Viscosity and Reynolds number 1.9

Re = ρ·V·c/μ = V·c/ν     τw = μ·(∂u/∂y)wall
  • Sea level: μ₀ = 1.79 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s, ν₀ = 1.46 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s. c = 1.5 m at 60 m/s → Re = 6.2 × 10⁶.
  • μ depends on T only (−17 % sea level → FL300). ρ falls faster, so ν rises with altitude.
  • Constant EAS: Re ∝ √σ/(μ/μ₀). FL300 → 0.74 × sea-level Re.
  • Turbulent layer: fuller profile, larger wall gradient, larger τw, higher resistance to separation.
  • Similarity requires equal Re and M together. A 1:10 model in the same fluid needs 10 × V for Re alone.

Moments and pitch equilibrium 1.10

ΣMcg = 0:   P·lt = Lw·a + |Mac|     Lw = W + P

P: tail download; lt: tail arm; a: distance of the wing aerodynamic centre aft of the CG. A couple (equal, opposite, parallel forces) has zero resultant and the same moment about every point; the moment about a section's aerodynamic centre is a couple.

02 Aerofoils & Lift Theory Aerofoils

A section is defined by three functions of angle of attack — cl(α), cd(α), CM(α) — set by camber line, thickness distribution and leading-edge radius. A finite wing modifies each through downwash and planform.

Pressure distribution 2.2

cl = ∫₀¹ (Cp,l − Cp,u) d(x/c)     xcp/c = ∫ ΔCp·(x/c) d(x/c) / cl
Shaded area between the curves = cl; its chordwise centroid = centre of pressure
  • Front stagnation point (Cp = +1) moves down and aft on the lower surface as α rises.
  • Upper-surface suction supplies about two-thirds or more of cl at moderate α.
  • A higher suction peak requires a steeper pressure recovery (dp/dx > 0) to the trailing edge; boundary-layer separation under that gradient defines the stall.
  • Symmetric section at α = 0: equal suction on both surfaces, cl = 0.
  • Load centroids: triangular (peak at LE) → c/3; uniform → c/2; flat plate, subsonic → c/4.

Section lift curve 2.3

cl = a₀·(α − α₀)     a₀ = 2π rad⁻¹ = 0.1097 deg⁻¹ (thin section)
  • Real sections: a₀ ≈ 0.10–0.11 deg⁻¹. Linear to about 0.85 cl,max; curvature follows as trailing-edge separation advances.
  • Post-stall: cl ↓, cd ↑ steeply, CM more nose-down (load moves toward mid-chord).
  • Camber shifts the curve left (α₀ more negative), leaves the slope unchanged, raises cl,max.
  • L′ = cl·q·c: cl 0.8, q 3 kPa, c 2 m → 4,800 N/m.

Aerodynamic centre and centre of pressure 2.4

CM,x = CM,ac + cl·(x − xac)/c
xcp = xac − (CM,ac/cl)·c     xac = xref − (dCM,ref/dcl)·c
  • Aerodynamic centre: the point about which dCM/dcl = 0. Subsonic ≈ c/4; supersonic ≈ c/2.
  • Positive camber: CM,ac < 0, so xcp lies aft of xac and moves forward toward it as cl rises; as cl → 0, xcp → ∞ (pure couple).
  • Symmetric: CM,ac = 0, xcp = xac at all α below the stall.
  • Mac = CM,ac·q·S·c ∝ VE²: doubling EAS quadruples the nose-down couple.

Thickness and leading edge 2.6

  • t/c ↑: rLE ↑ ∝ (t/c)², cl,max ↑ to a maximum near 12–15 % then ↓, form drag ↑, Mcrit ↓.
  • Thin section (≲ 9 %): thin-aerofoil stall — leading-edge bubble lengthens, gradual loss of slope.
  • Moderate (≈ 9–15 %): leading-edge stall — short bubble bursts, abrupt lift loss.
  • Thick (≳ 15 %): trailing-edge stall — separation advances forward from the trailing edge, gradual.
  • Maximum thickness aft: minimum-pressure point aft, longer favourable gradient, longer laminar run; a low-drag bucket in a narrow cl range, outside which transition moves forward and cd rises sharply.

Reynolds-number effects 2.7

  • Re ↑: cl,max ↑, stall α ↑, cd,min ↓ (cf ∝ Re−0.5 laminar, ∝ Re−0.2 turbulent).
  • Re ≲ 5 × 10⁵: laminar separation, transition in the free shear layer, turbulent reattachment — a laminar separation bubble; its bursting produces an abrupt stall.
  • Tapered wing: tip Re = λ × root Re → lower local cl,max outboard.
  • Roughness and contamination move transition forward: cl,max and stall α fall.

03 Lift and Drag Lift & Drag

Speed stability 3.8

dD/dV > 0 above Vmd: a speed loss reduces drag and the aircraft re-accelerates. dD/dV < 0 below Vmd: a speed loss raises drag and the deceleration diverges — the region of reversed command, where holding altitude at lower speed requires more thrust.

  • Sideslip raises drag: fuselage side force, associated induced and separation drag.

04 Boundary Layer Boundary Layer

No-slip at the wall creates a thin viscous layer in which shear stress is of the same order as inertia. Its state — laminar or turbulent — sets skin friction; its momentum against the imposed pressure gradient sets separation, and therefore form drag and the stall.

Structure and definitions 4.1

τw = μ·(∂u/∂y)wall     ∂p/∂y ≈ 0 across the layer
δ* = ∫(1 − u/U) dy    θ = ∫(u/U)(1 − u/U) dy    H = δ*/θ
  • δ: height where u = 0.99U. δ*: outward displacement of the effective body. θ: momentum deficit; H: profile fullness.
  • Static pressure is imposed by the outer inviscid flow and transmitted unchanged to the wall.
  • Turbulent layer: viscous sublayer (u ∝ y), logarithmic region, outer wake.

Pressure gradient and separation 4.4

  • dp/dx > 0 decelerates the near-wall fluid first; H rises; the profile inflects; separation at (∂u/∂y)wall = 0, reversed flow downstream.
  • Turbulent layers carry more near-wall momentum and survive larger pressure recoveries than laminar layers.
  • Laminar separation at low Re: free shear layer transitions and may reattach — a separation bubble; bursting gives abrupt stall.
  • Rising α steepens the recovery behind the suction peak: trailing-edge separation moves forward, lift slope falls, stall follows.
  • Separated region: no pressure recovery → loss of suction (lift) and a low-pressure wake (form drag).
  • Displacement at the trailing edge reduces effective camber and circulation: real lift slopes 0.10–0.11 deg⁻¹, below 2π rad⁻¹.

05 Stalling Stall

The stall is boundary-layer separation at the critical angle of attack, where CL = CL,max. The speed at which it occurs follows from the lift equation and varies with weight, load factor, configuration, CG, thrust and contamination; the critical angle does not vary with weight, load factor or altitude.

Warning and recovery 5.6

  • Buffet: separated wake striking wing and tail.
  • Lower-leading-edge vane: the stagnation point moves down and aft across it as α rises. An α-referenced warning is correct at any weight, bank and n; configuration still shifts the threshold.

07 Flight Controls: Primary Controls

A control surface changes camber and hence the force on its lifting surface; the moment produced scales with dynamic pressure and arm. The pilot feels the hinge moment. Balance, tabs, mass distribution and structural stiffness set force, stability and the speed limits of control.

Flutter: self-excited coupling of aerodynamic load with bending and torsion, divergent above the flutter speed. Mass balance puts the surface CG on or ahead of the hinge line. Ice or water aft of the hinge and control-circuit freeplay lower the flutter speed.

  • Reversal: aileron load aft of the elastic axis twists the wing; reversal q ∝ torsional stiffness (×1.44 → VR ×1.2). Outboard ailerons are locked out at high speed; inboard ailerons, roll spoilers or tailerons take over.

08 Secondary Controls & Trim Trim

Trim sets the flight condition at which control force is zero, by adjusting a hinge moment (tab) or tailplane incidence (stabiliser). It does not change stability or add control authority. Spoilers and speed brakes modify drag and lift for descent, roll and deceleration.

Trim tabs 8.1

b₂·δe + b₃·δt = 0  ⇒  δt = −(b₂/b₃)·δe
  • The tab deflects opposite to the surface it holds: elevator up ⇔ tab down.
  • |b₂| 0.006/deg, |b₃| 0.004/deg, δe 4° up → δt 6° down.
  • Tab trim consumes elevator travel in the trimmed direction.
  • Ground-adjustable tabs correct fixed rigging asymmetries; servo-tab systems trim by offsetting the tab datum.

Trimmable stabiliser 8.2

  • Incidence changes by jackscrew (irreversible); elevator faired with full travel; lower trim drag; large trim range for flap, Mach and CG changes.
  • Nose-up trim: stabiliser leading edge down, more negative incidence, more download.
  • Take-off setting from CG: forward CG → more nose-up trim.
  • Runaway: the stabiliser outweighs the elevator; opposing authority shrinks as mistrim grows.

Stick force and trim speed 8.3

F = K·(Vt² − V²)     |dF/dV|Vt = 2K·Vt
Push above trim speed, pull below; the gradient per knot grows with trim speed
  • Trimmed 150 kt: 50 N push at 180 kt → 88 N at 200 kt.
  • Trim moves the zero-force speed; stability sets the gradient. Released stick-free, a stable aircraft returns toward trim speed through the phugoid.
  • Tab and elevator hinge moments both scale with q.

09 Longitudinal Stability Pitch

Static stability is the initial tendency after a disturbance; dynamic stability is what happens next.

CG, neutral point, static margin 9.1

Moving the CG aft shortens the tailplane's moment arm, reducing stability, until it reaches the neutral point — zero stability — beyond which the aircraft is unstable in pitch. Static margin is the CG-to-neutral-point distance, as a percentage of mean aerodynamic chord.

Phugoid vs short-period 9.2

The phugoid is a slow, tens-of-seconds trade between speed and height at constant angle of attack. The short-period mode is a fast, heavily damped oscillation in angle of attack at constant speed. A tailplane download in trimmed flight balances the wing's own nose-down pitching moment; setting the tailplane at a slightly lower incidence than the wing ("longitudinal dihedral") helps ensure the wing stalls before the tailplane does.

10 Lateral & Directional Stability Roll & Yaw

Roll stability (dihedral effect) and yaw stability (the fin) interact — and the interaction has a name.

  • Dihedral, sweep, high-wing, keel surface — all add a rolling moment following a sideslip.
  • Fin (weathercock effect) — yaws the nose back into the relative airflow.
  • Dutch roll — coupled yaw/roll, worse when lateral stability is strong relative to directional; a yaw damper corrects it automatically.
  • Spiral instability — the opposite imbalance: bank slowly, progressively increases.
  • Anhedral — deliberately reduces excess lateral stability on already-stable (usually highly swept) designs.
The trade-off Strong directional stability relative to lateral favors spiral stability but worsens Dutch roll; the reverse favors Dutch roll damping but worsens spiral stability. Real aircraft sit at a deliberate compromise.

11 High-Speed: Compressibility Mach

At cruise Mach numbers, air can no longer be treated as incompressible.

Mach number = TAS ÷ local speed of sound, which depends only on temperature. Critical Mach number (MCRIT) is the freestream Mach at which local airflow over the wing first reaches Mach 1. Sweepback raises MCRIT, because only the flow component perpendicular to the leading edge drives the local acceleration. A thicker section or a higher lift coefficient (higher weight, higher angle of attack) both lower MCRIT.

12 High-Speed: Shockwaves & Buffet Transonic

Beyond MCRIT, a shockwave forms — and everything downstream of that shockwave changes.

A normal shock decelerates local flow abruptly from supersonic to subsonic; the sharp pressure rise across it can separate the boundary layer immediately behind it, producing Mach buffet and a steep rise in drag — wave drag. The rearward shift of center of pressure as shocks form produces an uncommanded nose-down moment, Mach tuck, countered by a Mach trim system. Design responses include the area rule (smoothing the total cross-section along the fuselage) and supercritical aerofoils (a flatter top, drooped trailing edge, to weaken the shock).

Coffin corner 12.1

At high altitude the two buffet boundaries converge, narrowing the safe speed margin between them

Control surface buzz is a related, smaller effect: an oscillating shock near a hinge line repeatedly separates and reattaches the local boundary layer, vibrating the surface.

13 Propellers Propellers

Each blade is a rotating aerofoil — everything about a propeller follows from that one fact.

Left-turning tendencies 13.1

EffectCause
Torque reactionNewton's third law — airframe rolls opposite to propeller rotation
Gyroscopic precessionA pitch/yaw force appears ~90° further round the direction of rotation
Asymmetric blade effect (P-factor)Descending blade meets a higher AoA at high power/high AoA
Slipstream effectSpiralling slipstream strikes the fin asymmetrically

Constant-speed propellers 13.2

A governor coarsens blade angle when RPM rises above the selected value (loading the engine, pulling RPM back down) and fines it when RPM falls — holding a selected RPM across a wide range of speed and power, unlike a fixed-pitch propeller efficient only near one design point (its advance ratio). Feathering turns the blades edge-on after an engine failure to minimise drag and stop windmilling, which otherwise adds substantial drag and can further damage the failed engine.

Blade count and tip speed 13.3

More, narrower blades absorb more power within a limited diameter, at some efficiency cost versus fewer, longer blades. Blade tip speed can reach transonic values from rotational speed alone — the same compressibility penalties (shock, drag, noise) that affect a fast wing. High density altitude reduces both propeller thrust and engine power together.

14 Loads & Limitations Limitations

How much load an airframe is built to take, and the speeds that protect it.

Load factor = lift ÷ weight. A level turn raises it because lift must also supply the horizontal, centripetal component. A V-n diagram bounds the envelope: the aerodynamic stall limit on one side, the structural limit load factor on the other. Manoeuvring speed (VA) is where the wing stalls exactly at limit load factor — it falls with weight, and above VA an abrupt full control input can overstress the structure before the wing stalls to protect it.

15 Windshear & Atmospheric Effects Windshear

A sudden change in wind speed or direction, and the atmospheric patterns that produce it.

A decreasing headwind (or increasing tailwind) on approach momentarily drops airspeed and lift, sinking the aircraft below path; an increasing headwind does the opposite, ballooning it above path — both because inertia keeps groundspeed from adjusting instantly. A microburst delivers the worst combination in sequence: increasing headwind, then a downdraft, then a sharply increasing tailwind, right as an aircraft is trying to climb away — which is why the standard response to significant windshear is a prompt go-around.

Mountain waves form when stable air is forced over terrain by a strong, roughly perpendicular wind, producing standing waves and severe turbulence well downwind. Clear air turbulence is most often tied to wind shear at the edges of the jet stream, with no cloud to give it away.
