02 Emergency Procedures Emergency Procedures

Section 3 of the POH. The checklists are short on purpose — memorize the shape of each one, not just the numbers, because under stress you'll reach for the shape first.

Airspeeds for emergency operation POH 3-3

Engine failure after takeoff60

Landing without power — flaps down60

Engine failure POH 3-3, 3-4

Three different checklists depending on when the failure happens, and the differences matter:

  • During the takeoff run — you're not flying yet. Throttle idle, brakes, flaps retract, mixture idle cut-off, ignition off, master off. The whole point is stopping on the runway.
  • Immediately after takeoff — 60 KIAS, mixture idle cut-off, fuel shutoff OFF, ignition OFF, flaps as required, master OFF. No restart attempt — you don't have the altitude to spare.
  • During flight — 60 KIAS, then carb heat ON, primer in and locked, fuel shutoff ON, mixture RICH, ignition BOTH (or START if the prop has stopped). Here you do try to restart, because you have altitude and time.
Why it matters The amplified procedures are blunt about the 180° turn back to the runway: altitude and airspeed are seldom sufficient. Plan straight ahead with small heading changes instead.

Fires POH 3-5, 3-6, 3-7

Four separate checklists — engine fire in flight, electrical fire in flight, cabin fire, wing fire — and each shuts down a different thing first. Engine fire cuts mixture/fuel/master and glides at 85 KIAS. Electrical fire cuts the master switch first, then works through switches one at a time to isolate the short. Both carry the same warning after discharging an extinguisher in a closed cabin: ventilate it.

Vacuum failure in clouds POH 3-10, 3-11

If the vacuum system fails and you're relying on the turn coordinator alone: a 180° turn is a standard rate left turn held 60 seconds. An emergency descent stabilizes at 500–800 ft/min at 70 KIAS. Recovering from a spiral dive brings the airspeed back to 70 KIAS as well — hands off the wheel, rudder only.

Spin recovery POH 3-12

  1. Ailerons neutral.
  2. Throttle idle.
  3. Full rudder opposite the direction of rotation.
  4. Just after the rudder hits the stop, control wheel briskly forward to break the stall.
  5. Hold until rotation stops.
  6. As rotation stops, neutralize rudder and recover smoothly from the dive.

Rough engine / low oil pressure POH 3-12, 3-13

Carburetor icing shows up as a gradual RPM loss — full throttle, full carb heat until smooth, then remove heat. Spark plug fouling and magneto trouble both show up the same way: switch momentarily from BOTH to L or R and look for an obvious power loss on one side. Total loss of oil pressure with rising oil temperature means an engine failure is likely imminent — reduce power and pick a field now.

Emergency Locator Transmitter (ELT) POH 9-7 to 9-9

Activates automatically at 5G or more when its switch is set to AUTO, transmitting on 121.5 and 243.0 MHz simultaneously. Check for inadvertent activation (a hard landing can trigger it with nothing actually wrong) by listening on 121.5 MHz — if you hear the tone, cycle the switch to OFF then back to AUTO to reset it.

03 Normal Procedures Normal Procedures

Section 4. This is the everyday flying — the numbers here are the ones your hands should know without thinking.

Before takeoff checks POH 4-7, 4-13, 4-14

  • Magneto check at 1700 RPM: drop should not exceed 125 RPM on either magneto, nor 50 RPM differential between them.
  • Alternator check: load the system 3–5 seconds with the landing light or flaps; the ammeter should stay within a needle width of its starting position.
  • Power check on the takeoff roll: engine should turn 2280–2380 RPM static, carb heat off, mixture leaned.

Takeoff flap technique POH 4-14

10° flaps cut the distance over a 50-ft obstacle by about 10% versus flaps up — but that's the ceiling: anything beyond 10° is not approved for takeoff at all. Left down until obstacles clear and 60 KIAS is reached, then retracted slowly.

Cruise & leaning POH 4-15, 4-16

Normal cruise sits between 55% and 75% power; the first 50 hours (or until oil consumption stabilizes) should stay in the upper part of that band, 65–75%, to seat the rings. Lean until RPM peaks and then drops 25–50 RPM for the book's fuel-flow figures. The recommended training-flight leaning technique can save up to 13% fuel over flying full rich throughout.

Stalls & spins POH 4-16, 4-17, 4-18

The stall horn sounds 5–10 knots before the actual stall, flaps up or down. For intentional spins: plan recoveries completed 4000 ft AGL or more, allow at least 1000 ft of loss for a 1-turn spin, and keep the ailerons dead neutral throughout — any aileron deflection in the spin direction can speed up the rotation. Spins with flaps extended are prohibited outright: recovery speeds could damage the flap/wing structure.

Balked landing & cold weather POH 4-18, 4-19

Balked landing (go-around): full power, flaps immediately to 20°, then slowly to full up once safe airspeed is reached. Preheat is generally required below -18°C (0°F) and recommended below -7°C (20°F).

Worth knowing Certificated noise level at max weight is 65.0 dB(A) — and the recommended altitude over noise-sensitive areas is 2000 ft AGL, weather permitting.

Wind correction is the same shape both directions: 10% distance change per 9 knots of headwind, or per 2 knots of tailwind (up to 10 kt). Grass runways add 15% to the takeoff ground roll, but a much larger 45% to the landing ground roll — soft ground bites much harder while braking than while accelerating.

Cruise performance POH 5-13

Published cruise speeds assume speed fairings are installed, adding about 2 knots. At 8000 ft, 2550 RPM, standard temperature: 75% BHP, 107 KTAS, 6.1 GPH.

Range & endurance reserve POH 5-3

Every range and endurance chart already bakes in a 45-minute reserve at 45% power — about 2.8 gallons. Don't plan a flight to the raw chart number and forget that the reserve is already gone from it.

Caution The book itself says mixture technique, engine/prop condition, and turbulence can swing range and endurance by 10% or more. Treat chart numbers as a planning baseline, not a guarantee.

05 Weight & Balance Weight & Balance

Section 1 and Section 6. The terminology here is worth memorizing precisely — the words are used exactly, and a test question hinges on the difference between them.

ModelStandard empty weightMax useful load

Wing loading 10.5 lbs/sq ft; power loading 15.2 lbs/hp.

Terminology that gets tested POH 1-10

  • Arm — horizontal distance from the datum to an item's CG.
  • Moment — weight × arm; divided by 1000 in this handbook purely to keep the numbers manageable.
  • Standard Empty Weight — includes unusable fuel, full fluids, full oil.
  • Basic Empty Weight — standard empty weight plus optional equipment.
  • Useful Load — takeoff weight minus basic empty weight.
  • Tare — the weight of the chocks/stands used while weighing, deducted from the scale reading.

Weighing procedure POH 6-3

All fuel and oil are drained first. Seats go to the most forward position, flaps fully retracted, controls neutral. Each wheel sits on a scale rated for at least 500 lbs. Once weight and moment/1000 are totaled, they're plotted on the CG Moment Envelope to confirm the loading actually falls inside it.

06 Powerplant Powerplant

Section 7. The O-235-L2C is about as simple as engines get — no turbo, no fuel injection, no electronic controls — which means the questions are really about how each subsystem physically works.

The engine POH 1-5, 7-14

Lycoming O-235-L2C: normally-aspirated, direct-drive, air-cooled, horizontally opposed, carbureted, four-cylinder, 233.3 cu in, rated 110 BHP at 2550 RPM. Starter, belt-driven alternator, and oil cooler mount on the front; dual magnetos sit on a rear accessory pad.

Instrument markings: tachometer green 1900–2550, red line 2550 RPM · oil temperature green 100–245°F, red line 245°F · oil pressure red line (min) 25 psi, green 60–90 psi, red line (max) 100 psi

Ignition & starting POH 7-16

Right magneto fires lower-right and upper-left plugs; left magneto fires lower-left and upper-right — dual ignition for more complete combustion, which is why normal operation stays on BOTH. Switch positions clockwise: OFF, R, L, BOTH, START.

Induction, carburetor & cooling POH 7-16, 7-17

Up-draft, float-type, fixed-jet carburetor with no accelerator pump — pumping the throttle while starting only causes excessive leaning, it doesn't help. Full carburetor heat at full throttle costs about 150–200 RPM. Cooling is ram-air only, no manual control; the winterization kit is advisable below -7°C (20°F).

Oil system POH 7-15, 7-16

Sump capacity 6 quarts (7 with a filter installed). Factory-fill corrosion-preventive oil is drained after the first 25 hours; cruise 65–75% power until 50 hours accumulate to seat the rings properly.

07 Fuel System Fuel System

Gravity feed, start to finish — there's no fuel pump anywhere in this system. That single fact explains most of how it behaves.

Fuel flows by gravity from both wing tanks through the shutoff valve and strainer to the carburetor; the tanks vent to each other and overboard through a check valve on the left tank

Quantity & unusable fuel POH 7-18

Two float-type transmitters feed two electric indicators. When an indicator reads empty, roughly 0.75 gallon still sits in that tank. Total unusable fuel across the airplane is about 1.5 gallons, a figure confirmed with 30-second full-rudder sideslips in the landing configuration — not just straight and level. Takeoffs have never been demonstrated with less than 2 gallons total (1 per tank) on board.

Reduced loading option Long range tanks can be serviced only to the bottom of the filler-neck indicator for a lighter fuel load — that gives 12 gallons per tank (12.25 usable total) instead of the full 19.5.

Preflight draining POH 4-5

Pull the strainer drain knob for about 4 seconds before the first flight of the day and after every refueling. If water shows up, keep draining at the strainer, both wing sumps, and the fuel line drain plug — one contaminated point usually means more water is still in the system.

08 Electrical System Electrical System

28 volts, one bus bar, one master switch with two independent halves. Simple enough to reason through from first principles once you know the split.

BAT controls all electrical power; ALT controls only the alternator. The HIGH VOLTAGE light shares the panel with the ammeter, just below it.

Power source POH 7-20

28-volt DC system: a 60-amp engine-driven alternator and a 14-amp hour battery (or 17-amp hour, if installed) on the right forward firewall. Everything runs through one bus bar except the ignition system, clock, and flight-hour recorder — those stay live even with the master switch off.

Master switch behavior POH 7-20

BAT (right half) controls all electrical power; ALT (left half) controls only the alternator. Leave ALT off long enough and the battery drains far enough to open the battery contactor and lock the alternator out of restarting — there's no shortcut back once that happens in flight.

Protection & lighting POH 7-22, 7-23

Most circuits use push-to-reset breakers under the engine controls. A handful — battery contactor closing, clock, flight-hour recorder — use fuses by the battery instead, since they're meant to stay live with the master off. If a breaker pops twice, the rule is simple: don't reset it again.

Brakes POH 7-19

Single-disc, hydraulically-actuated brake on each main wheel, each fed by its own master cylinder at the top of the rudder pedals — the left (pilot's) and right (copilot's) pedal sets are interconnected. Watch for the warning signs: gradual loss of braking, noisy or dragging brakes, spongy pedals, excessive travel. If one brake fails, use the other sparingly with opposite rudder to compensate — don't just stand on the good one.

Caution Never set the parking brake in freezing weather with accumulated moisture, or when the brakes are already overheated — either can seize them.

10 Flight Controls Flight Controls

Conventional three-axis, cable-and-pushrod, nothing powered. The flap system is the one piece with any complexity — it's electric, not mechanical, which surprises people expecting a manual lever.

Trim POH 7-8

A manual elevator trim tab, worked by a vertical trim wheel: forward rotates nose-down, aft rotates nose-up — easy to get backwards if you're used to a different airplane, so it's worth saying out loud once.

Wing flaps POH 7-9

Single-slot flaps, positioned by an electric switch lever with mechanical stops at 10° and 20° — going past 10° means deliberately sliding the lever sideways to clear the stop. Protected by a 15-amp breaker labeled FLAP. Takeoff is limited to 0°–10°; short-field and normal landings use up to 30°, below the 85-KIAS flap-extension limit.

Control locks POH 7-14

A single steel rod locks ailerons and elevator in neutral for parking, with a red CONTROL LOCK, REMOVE BEFORE STARTING ENGINE flag positioned right over the ignition switch — impossible to miss on preflight if it's actually installed correctly.

Balked landing flap sequence POH 4-9

Full power first, flaps to 20° immediately, then slowly to fully up only once a safe airspeed is established — never all at once.

11 Flight Instruments Flight Instruments

Two separate pneumatic systems feed most of the panel: pitot-static for the airspeed/VSI/altimeter trio, and engine-driven vacuum for the gyros. Knowing which system feeds which instrument is most of what gets tested here.

Pitot-static instruments POH 7-25, 7-26

Pitot tube on the lower left wing, static port on the lower left fuselage. Static pressure alone feeds the airspeed indicator, rate-of-climb indicator, and altimeter. The heated pitot option runs on a 15-amp breaker behind a switch labeled PITOT HT.

To read pressure altitude off the altimeter (for the true-airspeed ring): set the barometric scale to 29.92 momentarily, read the altitude, then restore the original setting.

Vacuum instruments POH 7-27, 7-28

An engine-driven vacuum pump supplies the attitude and directional indicators. Desired suction is 4.6 to 5.4 inches of mercury — below that range, treat both gyros as unreliable. The attitude indicator's bank scale is marked at 10°, 20°, 30°, 60°, and 90° each side. The directional indicator precesses over time, so it needs resetting against the magnetic compass before takeoff and periodically on a long flight.

Stall warning system POH 7-28

Purely pneumatic: an inlet in the left wing's leading edge feeds an air-operated horn as the low-pressure region migrates forward near the stall, sounding 5–10 knots before the actual break. Preflight check: cup a handkerchief over the inlet and apply suction — the horn should sound.

12 Airframe Airframe

All-metal, two-place, high-wing, semimonocoque — every word in that description shows up somewhere in a question, so it's worth being able to say what each one actually means.

The fuselage is semimonocoque: formed sheet-metal bulkheads, stringers, and skin sharing the structural load together, rather than a steel-tube truss carrying it alone. The externally braced wings carry the fuel tanks and use front/rear spars with sheet-metal ribs under an all-aluminum skin. The empennage is conventional: vertical stabilizer, rudder, horizontal stabilizer, elevator — with the rudder's trim tab ground-adjustable, and the entire right half of the elevator's trailing edge hinged to form the elevator trim tab.

Cabin POH 7-10, 7-13

Seats come in four-way or six-way adjustable versions — six-way adds 2 inches of height adjustment in 1-inch steps. A rear child's seat is available but non-adjustable. The baggage net uses six tie-down straps. Only the left door has a key lock. If a door pops open in flight from a bad latch, the fix is calm and specific: trim around 65 knots, shove the door out slightly, pull it shut firmly — landing is not required for this alone.

13 Propeller Propeller

No governor, no feathering, no constant-speed complexity — a fixed pitch propeller means RPM is purely a function of throttle and airspeed, which is exactly why the static-RPM check in Limitations matters so much: it's the one direct read you get on engine health.

McCauley 1A103/TCM6958: two-bladed, fixed-pitch, one-piece forged aluminum alloy, anodized against corrosion, 69 inches in diameter (worn limit 67.5 in).

Small leading-edge nicks near the tips are not cosmetic — they create stress concentrations that can grow into cracks, so they get dressed out promptly rather than left alone. On gravel, advance the throttle slowly so the airplane is already rolling before RPM builds, which throws gravel behind the blades instead of into them. During storage, the propeller gets turned by hand through five revolutions every seventh day — always treated as if the ignition were live, since a bad ground wire on either magneto could fire the engine.
